\title{The Effect of Program-Generated Deduction Data on
Instruction-Tuned Language Models}

\author{Anonymous authors}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Synthetic training data is almost always written by a language model, which
bounds the data by what the generating model already knows and reproduces the
errors that model makes. This paper studies the opposite kind of source. A
program samples a multi-hop deduction problem, solves it by forward chaining
and writes the solution out, so that every document is correct by construction
and no teacher model takes part. Replacing one percent of an ordinary
instruction mixture with such documents takes Qwen2.5-7B from writing no
derivation that a checker accepts to writing 51-line derivations that a checker
accepts in more than nine cases out of ten, and the ability carries to chains
about twice as long as the longest chain in training. Accuracy on held-out
deduction benchmarks requires a larger share and then stops improving. At five
percent, accuracy on ProofWriter at inference depth 3 rises from 39.3 to 50.0
percent, English-rendered derivations reach 53.2 percent at ten percent of the
mixture, and accuracy on FOLIO rises from 51.4 to 57.8 percent. The improvement
turns out to have a specific cause. Ordinary instruction tuning leaves the
model asserting that a claim holds in three cases out of four, and the model
answers a claim and its negation identically on more than a third of the items.
The generated derivations remove that bias, and the entire benchmark gain lies
in the claims that are negated and false, where accuracy rises from 17.6 to
56.9 percent. The defect therefore belongs to the instruction mixture and a
model-written corpus is the wrong instrument for removing it, since the writer
is liable to share the defect. A small quantity of cheap and verifiable data
with no teacher in it is enough.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

An instruction-tuned language model can hold everything a task requires and
still answer by a rule that ignores part of the input. The model we start from
answers that a claim holds in three cases out of four on ProofWriter
\citep{tafjord2021proofwriter}, and on the claims that are negated and false it
is correct 17.6 percent of the time, which is worse than a coin. Repairing a
defect of this kind calls for training data in which the defect is absent.
Synthetic text is the usual way to obtain training data on demand, and in
nearly every published recipe another language model writes that text.
Textbook-style corpora \citep{gunasekar2023textbooks, eldan2023tinystories},
rephrased web documents \citep{maini2024rephrasing, maini2025beyondweb,
su2025nemotroncc} and prompted expansions of a seed corpus
\citep{yang2025synthetic} all take this form. Such a corpus inherits the errors
of its writer, and repeated training on it narrows the distribution of the
trained model \citep{shumailov2024collapse, dohmatob2025strong}, which makes it
a poor instrument for removing a bias the writer is liable to share.

A program is the alternative source. A few hundred lines of Python sample a
multi-hop deduction problem, solve it and write the solution out, which yields
documents that are correct by construction, unlimited in number and free of any
teacher model. Verified data of this kind has been used at scale inside narrow
systems \citep{feng2025verification, trinh2024alphageometry}. The objection to
using it in a general instruction mixture is that the documents are trivial.
They describe abstract constants that carry colour and direction predicates,
they contain no world knowledge, and they share four words with the two hundred
most frequent words of the benchmarks on which we evaluate.
Figure~\ref{fig:overview-a} shows one such document. This paper asks whether
material of that kind changes a general instruction-tuned model at all, how
much of it is needed, and what the change consists of.

\input{fig_design}

The experiment holds the size of the training set fixed and varies only the
content of a slice of it. We fine-tune Qwen2.5-7B \citep{qwen2025qwen25} on a fixed
instruction mixture in which 1, 5, 10 or 25 percent of the examples have been
replaced by generated derivations, so that every condition trains on the same
number of examples and no condition benefits from seeing more data. The
derivations appear in two notations drawn from the same latent proofs, one
formal and one in controlled English, which isolates the effect of the notation
from the effect of the content.

Two effects emerge, and they appear at different shares of the mixture. One
percent, which amounts to a thousand documents, is enough for the model to
write a derivation that a mechanical checker accepts, at chain lengths about
twice the longest one in training, where a model trained on the same mixture
without the replacement writes no acceptable derivation in six hundred
attempts. Benchmark accuracy needs a larger share. At five percent, accuracy
on ProofWriter at inference depth 3 rises from 39.3 to 50.0 percent, ten
percent of English-rendered derivations reaches 53.2 percent, and accuracy on
FOLIO \citep{han2024folio} rises by 6.4 points. Figure~\ref{fig:overview-b}
shows the curve, which is flat above five percent. On seven general benchmarks
the conditions stay close to the control, and the two that move by more than
its seed-to-seed range, ARC-Challenge and LogiQA, move upward.

The origin of the gain is narrower than the headline numbers suggest, and more
useful. Splitting the held-out problems by the polarity of the claim and by the
gold label puts the entire improvement in one of the four resulting cells, the
claims that are negated and false, where accuracy rises from 17.6 to 56.9
percent as the share grows. The other three cells move by a few points at most,
and the probability of answering ``true'' falls monotonically from 0.73 to
0.50. What the generated derivations supply is therefore not general reasoning
ability but the absent half of a decision rule, the half in which a chain of
inferences terminates in a refusal. The instruction corpus leaves that half
underdetermined, no amount of data written by a model that shares the omission
will determine it, and a thousand documents from a program are enough. The
same construction applies wherever a defect can be characterised well enough
for a program to generate its cure, which is a broader class than deduction.

Our contributions are the following.

\begin{itemize}
\item A measurement of how much program-generated deduction data an instruction
      mixture needs. One percent installs the ability to write checkable
      derivations, five percent gives the full benchmark gain, and larger
      shares add nothing measurable.
\item An account of what the gain consists of. It is the repair of an
      affirmative answer prior left behind by instruction tuning, and it is
      confined to a single cell of a polarity by label split of the evaluation
      set.
\item An examination of the derivations the model writes. A forward-chaining
      checker accepts them, and we report both the setting in which the checker
      converts sampling into accuracy without an answer key and the setting in
      which it fails to do so.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Synthetic data written by models.} Most synthetic pretraining
corpora are produced by prompting a language model, either to write documents
in a chosen style \citep{gunasekar2023textbooks, eldan2023tinystories} or to
rewrite existing text from the web \citep{maini2024rephrasing,
maini2025beyondweb, su2025nemotroncc}. The quality of the result is bounded by
the generating model, and repeated training on such text degrades the
distribution of the trained model \citep{shumailov2024collapse,
dohmatob2025strong}. The source studied here contains no model at all.

\paragraph{Synthetic deduction corpora.} Several corpora present deduction
problems to language models for training or for evaluation, among them
RuleTaker and ProofWriter \citep{clark2020transformers, tafjord2021proofwriter},
PrOntoQA \citep{saparov2023language} and FLD \citep{morishita2023fld,
morishita2024alt}. These corpora render their proofs in English.
\citet{zhou2025finelogic} compare symbolic supervision with English supervision
on matched problems and report that English supervision generalises further.
Both renderings here come from a single latent proof, which holds the problem
and the answer fixed while the notation varies, and the ordering of the two
turns out to depend on the depth of the training proofs.

\paragraph{Verifiable rewards and checkable traces.} Training a model against a
verifier has become a standard recipe in mathematics and in code
\citep{lambert2025tulu, yue2025does}, and formal systems allow a machine to
check a proof \citep{trinh2024alphageometry}. A small forward-chaining program
checks the derivations generated here, which makes it possible to ask whether a
checker can select a correct sample without an answer key.

\paragraph{Answer priors in tuned models.} Instruction-tuned models acquire
response biases that are separable from their underlying competence
\citep{ouyang2022training}, and negation in particular remains difficult for
language models \citep{kassner2020negated, hosseini2021understanding,
truong2023language}. The mechanistic result below connects these two lines of
work. The pathology that synthetic deduction data repairs is an affirmative
answer prior installed by instruction tuning, and the repair appears as a
change in that prior while accuracy on the remaining items stays where it was.

\section{Generated data}
\label{sec:data}

The generator samples a set of constants, a set of unary predicates and a set
of implication rules, chooses a target chain length, and constructs a problem
whose answer follows from the premises by that many applications of modus
ponens. It then solves the problem by forward chaining and writes the solution
out. No language model participates in any step, and every document is correct
by construction. The vocabulary is deliberately impoverished. Constants are
named $c_0$ through $c_{25}$ and predicates are drawn from a list of colour and
direction words, so that the documents carry no world knowledge.

Each latent proof admits two renderings, both of which appear in
Figure~\ref{fig:overview-a}. The formal rendering declares the constants and
the predicate symbols, states the premises as implications between atoms, and
lists the proof as numbered lines together with the rule that justifies each
line. The English rendering states the same premises and the same proof as
ordinary sentences. The prompt, the answer and the proof graph are common to
the two renderings, so a comparison between them isolates notation.

We build every training mixture by replacement. A condition with share $p$
contains
$(1-p)\cdot 10^5$ examples of the instruction corpus and $p \cdot 10^5$
rendered derivations, so that all conditions train on exactly $10^5$ examples
and differ only in the content of the replaced slice. The evaluation split of
every mixture is drawn from the instruction corpus alone. This design removes
the most common confound in experiments on data augmentation, which is that the
treated condition simply sees more data than the control.

We audit the overlap between the generated documents and the evaluation sets
and find no evaluation item that shares an eight-gram with any training
derivation. The two hundred most frequent words of the two corpora overlap in
four words, and the words on which the benchmarks rely, among them ``not'',
``someone'' and the animal nouns of ProofWriter, never occur in the training
derivations.

\section{Experiments}

We fine-tune Qwen2.5-7B on each mixture for one epoch with a global batch of
128 sequences and a context window of 8{,}192 tokens. The window follows from
measurement of the corpus, since the longest rendered derivation in the
25-hop band occupies 7{,}700 tokens. A run aborts before it starts if more than
0.5 percent of its examples would be truncated, so no condition trains on a
derivation whose conclusion has been cut off. We train three random seeds for every
configuration and report all of them.
Appendix~\ref{sec:sweep-details} gives the remaining hyperparameters.

We evaluate on ProofWriter under the open-world assumption at inference depths
0 through 5, on FOLIO, on the deduction problems of the generator at chain
lengths 5 through 25, and on seven general benchmarks. For the problems of the
generator we prompt the model in the document format on which it was trained,
since a derivation has to be requested before a checker can read it.

\section{Results}

Table~\ref{tab:dose} reports every condition, and the two effects separate
cleanly.

The ability to write a derivation that a checker accepts is already complete at
one percent of the mixture. A model trained without derivations writes none in
six hundred attempts, a model trained with a thousand of them writes a
derivation in every attempt, and a forward-chaining checker accepts more than
nine in ten of those derivations at chain length 25. The proofs run to 51 lines
at that depth, which is two lines for every hop together with the premises, and
the ability extends to chains of length 45, about twice the longest chain in
training, where the checker still accepts nine in ten.

Accuracy on held-out benchmarks requires a larger share. At one percent it
rises by about a point on ProofWriter at depth 3, which falls inside the spread
between seeds. At five percent it rises from 39.3 to 50.0 percent, and at ten
percent of English-rendered derivations it reaches 53.2 percent. Every seed of
every condition at five percent or more scores above every seed of the control,
and the spread among the three control seeds is 1.4 points. Accuracy on FOLIO
rises from 51.4 to 57.8 percent. Above five percent the curve is flat, and a
condition with a quarter of the mixture replaced scores 51.6 percent, inside
the noise of the five percent condition.

Table~\ref{tab:general} in the appendix reports eight general benchmarks. Five of them stay
inside the range spanned by the three control seeds. The three that leave it
move upward, BIG-Bench Hard by up to 2.7 points against a control range of
0.9, ARC-Challenge by up to 2.8 against 0.8 and LogiQA by up to 2.1 against
0.6, while accuracy on GSM8K falls by at most 1.3 points against a control
range of 0.3. GPQA-Diamond, which we score outside the general
suite, shows no measurable effect, since its 198 items give the control a seed
spread of 3.5 points and no condition departs from the control by more than
that.

Multi-hop question answering moves much less. On HotpotQA, 2WikiMultihopQA and
MuSiQue, reported in Table~\ref{tab:multihop} of the appendix, the English
rendering at ten percent gains 1.1, 1.9 and 1.7 points. Splitting
2WikiMultihopQA by question type locates even that. The 192 comparison
questions, which retrieve two facts and then compare them, gain 6.3 points,
while the 372 bridge questions, which chain retrievals and need no inference,
do not move, and weighting the first by its share accounts for the movement of
the benchmark as a whole. MMLU marks the other boundary. Its twelve logic and
mathematics subjects lose 1.6 points where the other forty-five gain 0.1, so
subject matter that sounds deductive predicts nothing. What the replacement
improves is the propagation of a value through a chain at inference time.

A prompt that invites the trained format exposes a cost. Asked for the answer
inside the tags the generated documents use, the ten percent formal condition
falls to 11.1 percent F1 on HotpotQA against 54.1 for the control, because it
opens a predicate legend over the entities of the passage and exhausts its
budget before answering, while at one percent the same prompt is the best of
all conditions.

The 27 subtasks of BIG-Bench Hard differ in whether they require the operation
the generated data contains, so Table~\ref{tab:bbh} splits them into the eight
whose answer follows by propagating a truth value or a state along a sequence
of steps and the nineteen that do not. The control scores 62.2 on the first
group and 66.1 on the second, so it is worse at exactly what the derivations
teach. The replacement lifts the first group to 68.9 and leaves the second
within a point, and on web of lies, which chains assertions about who tells the
truth through negations, accuracy rises from 85.5 to 97.6 percent and grows
monotonically with the share. Word sorting, navigation and object counting do
not move. The mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:analysis} therefore predicts where
a benchmark we did not design will improve.

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{Accuracy by claim polarity and gold label}

ProofWriter presents a theory together with a claim and asks whether the claim
is true, false or unknown. Two properties of an item are readable off the
prompt, whether the claim is negated and what the gold label is, and we report
accuracy in each of the four resulting cells for the decidable items in
Table~\ref{tab:mechanism}.

The control answers correctly on 98.5 percent of the claims that are negated
and true, on 68 percent of the claims that are positive and false, and on 75
percent of the claims that are positive and true. On the claims that are
negated and false it answers correctly on 17.6 percent. A model that fails to
reason would not produce this pattern. The control asserts that the claim
holds, it does so in three cases out of four across the whole evaluation set,
and the gold labels are close to balanced.

The generated derivations correct this behaviour. Accuracy on the negated and
false claims rises from 17.6 to 56.9 percent as the share grows, the
probability of answering ``true'' falls monotonically from 0.73 to 0.50, and
the three remaining cells move by a few points at most. The large cell of
positive and true claims drifts downward by about eight points, which is the
direction a correction of an affirmative prior predicts. The improvement is a
change in a decision rule, and the split makes that change directly visible.

\subsection{Where the change sits in the model's scores}
\label{sec:probes}

The polarity split shows which items change, and three probes show what changes
inside the model's scores. We run them on the midtrained and instruction-tuned
arms of Section~\ref{sec:midtraining}, which carry a length-matched control
that the sweep does not have. The first ranks decidable items by the
log-likelihood margin between ``true'' and ``false''. The control ranks well
inside each polarity, at 0.872 and 0.860, and far worse pooled, at 0.662,
because the margin of every negated claim is displaced toward ``true''. The
derivation arms remove most of that displacement, raising the pooled figure by
0.05 for the formal rendering and 0.11 for the English one, while the
length-matched control moves nothing. Appendix~\ref{sec:probe-table} gives the
table.

Two further probes intervene on the item instead of reading scores. Toggling
the polarity of the claim, the control answers both versions identically on
37.6 percent of decidable items against 28.5 for the formal arm and 24.2 for
the English one. Removing the premises that support the claim collapses most of
the extra negative answer, from 0.53 to 0.27 for the formal arm, which a
premise-grounded answer predicts, while the English arm retains more of it.
Appendix~\ref{sec:perturbation} gives both in full.

\subsection{The same mechanism on a benchmark we did not design}

Web of lies repeats the analysis of Section~\ref{sec:analysis} outside our own
evaluation. It states a chain of assertions about who tells the truth and asks
whether the last person does, and Table~\ref{tab:wol} of the appendix reports
the same quantities as Table~\ref{tab:mechanism}.

The control carries the same affirmative prior. It answers ``yes'' on 56.7
percent of items while 48.8 percent are ``yes'', and it is right on 93.2
percent of the positive items against 78.1 of the negative ones. The formal
conditions move the answer rate to the gold rate and lift the negative cell to
97.9 percent.

The same samples rule out two alternative explanations. Every condition emits
the answer phrase on every item, so this is not compliance with the extractor,
and every condition writes about 129 words, so it is not deliberation.

The task supports a stronger statement than a corrected prior, because the
model writes one numbered line per person and each line states the truth value
it is carrying. Reading that value at every position and comparing it with the
value the chain implies gives Table~\ref{tab:steps}. Every condition is correct
at the second position, and the control then decays, holding the right value on
98.1, 92.1 and 85.5 percent of items at the third, fourth and fifth, where the
ten percent formal condition holds 99.3, 97.7 and 97.6. The two rightmost
columns close the account, since every condition answers correctly on
essentially every item whose intermediate values are all correct and almost
never on an item where one is wrong. Accuracy here is the probability of
carrying the value to the end, and the replacement raises it from 0.849 to
0.975 without changing what the model does with the value once it has it.

FOLIO gives a third instance. The control answers ``true'' on 51.7 percent of
its items while 35.5 percent are true, and the replacement raises the false
items from 32.3 to 45.2 percent and the uncertain items from 38.6 to 52.2 while
the true ones stay put. Appendix~\ref{sec:folio-table} gives the split. Object tracking has no such
prior to repair, since its answer is one of several positions, and there the
treated models simply propagate the state correctly more often, 76.3 percent
against 62.3.


\subsection{Control conditions}

Three controls bound what else could produce the effect.

The first concerns length and domain. In a midtraining study on the same generator we
replaced the same share of the corpus with ordinary documents drawn from the
pretraining mixture and matched to the derivations in length distribution. That
condition is indistinguishable from the control on ProofWriter, with a paired
test giving $p=0.62$ and $p=1.00$ in two seeds, while the derivation condition
gains 12.6 points. Length and domain therefore do not explain the gain.

The second concerns memorisation, which the overlap audit of
Section~\ref{sec:data} rules out.

The third concerns the role of deductive order. A further midtraining condition that we run
uses the same derivations with the lines of each proof shuffled, which
preserves every token, the vocabulary, the length and the conclusion, and
destroys only the order in which the facts follow from one another. This
condition is in training at the time of writing and will be reported with the
same measures.

\subsection{Chain-of-thought prompting}

If the improvement consisted of the model reasoning more, asking the control to
reason aloud would close the gap, and it does not. Under a prompt that requests
a chain of reasoning before the answer, the control reaches 39.9 percent on
ProofWriter at inference depth 3, while the formal conditions reach 45.3, 49.5
and 55.8 percent at five, ten and 25 percent of the mixture and the English
conditions reach 44.1, 43.8 and 47.0. The advantage therefore grows with the
share in exactly the regime where a well-prompted baseline is the natural
objection, and it is the largest gap we measure anywhere, which also makes the
formal rendering the stronger one once the model reasons aloud.

\section{Checking the derivations}

A derivation written in a fixed format can be checked by a program. Our checker
reads the premises of an item and accepts a proof line when the line
is a premise or follows from lines already established. It reads both
notations, translating formal lines through the predicate legend that the model
itself writes. On ProofWriter it reproduces the published gold label on 99, 95,
91, 91 and 84 percent of the items at inference depths 0 through 5, and its
residual errors are answers of ``unknown'' on items whose gold label is
decided, so the checker under-credits the model.

On the problems of the generator the checker behaves almost as an oracle. A
derivation it accepts carries the correct answer in 99 percent of samples, and
selecting the first accepted derivation out of sixteen matches the accuracy of
asking whether any of them is correct, without reading a gold answer.

On ProofWriter the same rule is much weaker. Among sixteen derivations per item
sampled at temperature 0.8, between 28 and 37 percent carry a proof the checker
accepts, against none for the control, and an accepted derivation is correct
between 53 and 69 percent of the time, so checker selection trails majority
voting. A third of the items ask whether a claim is underivable, which a
forward chain never demonstrates, so a checker becomes useful only once it is
complete with respect to the task.

\section{Notation}

The two notations transfer about equally on the deduction benchmarks, with the
English rendering ahead at ten percent and the formal rendering ahead at five.
A controlled study on the generator alone shows that the ordering there is not
fixed and that the depth of the training proofs decides it, since English
supervision generalises further up to 20 training hops and formal supervision
reverses the ordering at 25, reaching $75.0\pm9.5$ percent out-of-distribution
accuracy against $17.8\pm4.7$. Appendix~\ref{sec:crossover} reports that study
with its controls.

The general benchmarks separate the two notations where the deduction
benchmarks do not. On the chain subtasks of BIG-Bench Hard in
Table~\ref{tab:bbh}, the formal rendering gains 3.9 to 6.7 points over the
control at every share while the English rendering gains 1.0 to 4.2, and on web
of lies the formal rendering reaches 97.6 percent against 87.5. The advantage of
the formal rendering therefore appears wherever the supervision has to transfer
as a procedure for propagating truth values, and it is absent on ProofWriter,
where the English rendering is ahead at ten percent. A practitioner choosing
between them should weigh which of the two kinds of transfer the application
needs.

\section{Midtraining}
\label{sec:midtraining}

The same derivations can enter during midtraining instead. Midtraining
Qwen2.5-7B on 2.5 billion tokens with a tenth of the mixture replaced, then
instruction tuning every condition alike, produces the same gain on ProofWriter
of 12.6 points. That route is the more expensive one and buys no more, since
the instruction-tuning route reaches the same accuracy, generalises further on
long chains, and avoids the two to three points the midtraining route loses on
LogiQA. Applying both in sequence adds nothing to either.
Appendix~\ref{sec:midtrain-detail} reports the comparison, including the one
ability midtraining leaves behind, which is that the midtrained model writes
checkable proofs when prompted in the format of its training documents and
writes none after ordinary instruction tuning.

\section{Checkable traces as a substrate for chain of thought}

A chain of thought is ordinarily assessed by reading it, and its steps carry no
form that a program can evaluate. The derivations studied here differ, and the
difference is cheap. One percent of an instruction mixture installs the format,
the model keeps writing in it at twice the training chain length, and no
general benchmark loses more than 1.3 points.

Once the format is present, validity and correctness become coupled. On the
problems of the generator, a derivation the checker accepts carries the correct
answer in 99 percent of samples, and a selection rule that never reads a gold
answer recovers the accuracy of sixteen-sample coverage. A coupling of that
strength converts a trace a reader has to trust into a claim a machine can
test, and it supplies a training signal that requires no reference answer,
which is the property that makes reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards
practical in mathematics and in code \citep{lambert2025tulu}.

The coupling weakens in proportion to the incompleteness of the checker. On
ProofWriter an accepted derivation is correct between 53 and 69 percent of the
time, and on GSM8K the coupling nearly disappears, since arithmetic validity
separates correct answers from incorrect ones by 3 to 8 points against the 99
it achieves on the generator's own problems. A wrong answer there usually
follows from correct arithmetic inside a wrong plan.

These observations point to a direction for further work. The substrate is
inexpensive, it survives contact with a general instruction mixture, and where
the checker is complete it makes a model's reasoning auditable step by step.
What is missing is a rendering whose checker covers the claims that tasks
actually turn on, which includes the plan a solution follows and the assertion
that something does not follow, beyond the individual inference steps that a
forward chain already settles. We
regard the construction of such renderings as the most promising continuation
of this work, because what a complete checker buys goes beyond accuracy to an
explanation whose validity a machine can establish.

\section{Limitations}

The gains we report are confined to deduction. ProofWriter and FOLIO
improve, the seven general benchmarks move by at most a few points, and we have
no evidence that bears on a task that does not ask for a chain of inferences.

The mechanism we identify is a correction of an answer prior, which is a
narrower claim than an improvement in reasoning capability, and a model whose
prior is already balanced may gain less than the model we study.

Our checker is sound and incomplete, since it cannot certify that a fact is
underivable, which limits checker-based selection on open-world benchmarks. We
also train one model family at one scale, and the prior the derivations repair
is a property of the instruction corpus we use.

\section{Conclusion}

A program that writes deduction problems and their solutions produces training
data with no teacher model in it, and a small quantity of it changes what an
instruction-tuned model does. One percent installs checkable derivations at
twice the training chain length and five percent gives the full benchmark gain.
What improves is the probability of carrying a value to the end of a chain.
