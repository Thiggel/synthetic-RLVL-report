\title{What Program-Generated Deduction Data Teaches an
Instruction-Tuned Language Model}

\author{Anonymous authors}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Synthetic training data is almost always written by a language model, which
bounds the data by what the generating model already knows and reproduces the
errors that model makes. This paper studies a source that contains no model at
all. A program samples a multi-hop deduction problem, solves it by forward
chaining and writes the solution out, so that every document is correct by
construction. We replace a small share of an ordinary instruction mixture with
such documents and ask what the model that results has learned. Two things,
and they come apart under every test we apply. The first is a procedure. One
percent of the mixture takes a model from writing no derivation that a checker
accepts to writing 51-line derivations accepted in more than nine cases out of
ten, at chain lengths twice those seen in training, and this holds across four
dense model families and three scales, where no instruction corpus alone
produces a single accepted derivation below 14B parameters. It does not hold
on a mixture of experts. The
second is a correction of the answer prior that instruction tuning installs. On
Qwen2.5-7B accuracy on ProofWriter rises from 39.3 to 53.2 percent and the
entire gain lies in negated claims that are false, where the control answers
``true''. We show by manipulation that the prior belongs to the instruction
corpus, since a second corpus installs the opposite prior and the same
derivations correct it, and we show that the correction can reverse sign on
models whose prior already leans the other way, and that on the sparse model
both renderings harm accuracy. The two effects separate on
three further axes. Shuffling the proof lines preserves most of the accuracy
gain on ProofWriter and destroys it on FOLIO, training depth decides how far
the procedure extends but leaves accuracy untouched, and training on
derivations alone yields a perfect derivation writer that cannot answer a
question.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

An instruction-tuned language model can hold everything a task requires and
still answer by a rule that ignores part of the input. The model we start from
answers that a claim holds in three cases out of four on ProofWriter
\citep{tafjord2021proofwriter}, and on the claims that are negated and false it
is correct 17.6 percent of the time. Repairing a defect of this kind calls for
training data in which the defect is absent, and synthetic text is the usual
way to obtain training data on demand. In nearly every published recipe
another language model writes that text. Textbook-style corpora
\citep{gunasekar2023textbooks, eldan2023tinystories}, rephrased web documents
\citep{maini2024rephrasing, maini2025beyondweb, su2025nemotroncc} and prompted
expansions of a seed corpus \citep{yang2025synthetic} all take this form. Such a
corpus inherits the errors of its writer, and repeated training on it narrows
the distribution of the trained model \citep{shumailov2024collapse,
dohmatob2025strong}, which makes it a poor instrument for removing a bias the
writer is liable to share.

A program is the alternative source. A few hundred lines of Python sample a
multi-hop deduction problem, solve it and write the solution out, which yields
documents that are correct by construction, unlimited in number and free of any
teacher model. The objection to using them in a general instruction mixture is
that they are trivial. They describe abstract constants that carry colour and
direction predicates, they contain no world knowledge, and they share four
words with the two hundred most frequent words of the benchmarks on which we
evaluate. Figure~\ref{fig:overview-a} shows one such document. This paper asks
what a model learns from them, and it finds two separable things.

\input{fig_design}

The first is a procedure. We fine-tune a base model on a fixed instruction
mixture in which one, five, ten or twenty-five percent of the examples have
been replaced by generated derivations, so that every condition trains on the
same number of examples. At one percent, which amounts to a thousand documents,
the model writes a derivation that a mechanical checker accepts on more than
nine problems in ten at chain length 25, and the ability extends to chains
twice that long. We repeat the treatment on Qwen2.5 at 3B, 7B and 14B
parameters, on Llama-3.1-8B, on OLMo-2-7B and on a mixture of experts. The procedure
appears in full on the four dense Qwen and Llama models, in part on OLMo-2,
and not at all on the mixture of experts. No control in any family below 14B
writes a single accepted derivation.

The second is a correction of the answer prior. On Qwen2.5-7B accuracy on
ProofWriter at inference depth 3 rises from 39.3 to 53.2 percent and on FOLIO
\citep{han2024folio} from 51.4 to 57.8, and splitting the items by the polarity
of the claim and the gold label places the entire gain in one of the four
cells, the claims that are negated and false. The control asserts them. We
show that the prior comes from the instruction corpus by training the same base
model on a second corpus, which installs the opposite prior, and observing that
the same derivations correct it in the same direction. We also show that the
correction is not a free gain, since on models whose prior already leans away
from ``true'' the formal rendering moves the distribution further out and
accuracy falls, by 12 points on OLMo-2 and 26 on the mixture of experts, while
the English rendering helps every dense model we measure and fails only on the
sparse one, where neither rendering installs the procedure.

The two effects separate under three further manipulations. Shuffling the
lines of every training proof, which preserves the tokens and the conclusions
and destroys the order in which facts follow, keeps three fifths of the
ProofWriter gain and almost none of the FOLIO gain. Training on proofs of five
hops installs a procedure that fails at fifteen, while accuracy is unchanged.
Training on derivations alone, with no instruction data, produces a model that
writes a perfect derivation on every generated problem and cannot answer a
question. The paper's contribution is therefore an account of what this data
does, with its boundaries measured on both sides. It is not a recipe for
engineering capabilities in general, since four further generators built to
match specific target tasks improve none of them.

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Synthetic data written by models.} Most synthetic pretraining
corpora are produced by prompting a language model, either to write documents
in a chosen style \citep{gunasekar2023textbooks, eldan2023tinystories} or to
rewrite existing text from the web \citep{maini2024rephrasing,
maini2025beyondweb, su2025nemotroncc}. The quality of the result is bounded by
the generating model, and repeated training on such text degrades the
distribution of the trained model \citep{shumailov2024collapse,
dohmatob2025strong}. The source studied here contains no model at all.

\paragraph{Synthetic deduction corpora.} Several corpora present deduction
problems to language models for training or for evaluation, among them
RuleTaker and ProofWriter \citep{clark2020transformers, tafjord2021proofwriter},
PrOntoQA \citep{saparov2023language} and FLD \citep{morishita2023fld,
morishita2024alt}. These corpora render their proofs in English.
\citet{zhou2025finelogic} compare symbolic supervision with English supervision
on matched problems and report that English supervision generalises further.
Both renderings here come from a single latent proof, and we find that the
English rendering extrapolates beyond its training depth where the formal one
does not, and that it helps every dense model where the formal one helps two of six.

\paragraph{Verifiable rewards and checkable traces.} Training a model against a
verifier has become a standard recipe in mathematics and in code
\citep{lambert2025tulu, yue2025does}, and formal systems allow a machine to
check a proof \citep{trinh2024alphageometry}. A small forward-chaining program
checks the derivations generated here.

\paragraph{Answer priors in tuned models.} Instruction-tuned models acquire
response biases that are separable from their underlying competence
\citep{ouyang2022training}, and negation in particular remains difficult for
language models \citep{kassner2020negated, hosseini2021understanding,
truong2023language}. We locate one such bias, show by manipulation that the
instruction corpus installs it, and show what corrects it.

\section{Generated data}
\label{sec:data}

The generator samples a set of constants, a set of unary predicates and a set
of implication rules, chooses a target chain length, and constructs a problem
whose answer follows from the premises by that many applications of modus
ponens. It then solves the problem by forward chaining and writes the solution
out. No language model participates in any step, and every document is correct
by construction. The vocabulary is deliberately impoverished. Constants are
named $c_0$ through $c_{25}$ and predicates are drawn from a list of colour and
direction words, so that the documents carry no world knowledge.

Each latent proof admits two renderings, both of which appear in
Figure~\ref{fig:overview-a}. The formal rendering declares the constants and
the predicate symbols, states the premises as implications between atoms, and
lists the proof as numbered lines together with the rule that justifies each
line. The English rendering states the same premises and the same proof as
ordinary sentences. The prompt, the answer and the proof graph are common to
the two renderings, so a comparison between them isolates notation.

We build every training mixture by replacement. A condition with share $p$
contains $(1-p)\cdot 10^5$ examples of the instruction corpus and $p \cdot
10^5$ rendered derivations, so that all conditions train on exactly $10^5$
examples and differ only in the content of the replaced slice. The evaluation
split of every mixture is drawn from the instruction corpus alone. This design
removes the most common confound in experiments on data augmentation, which is
that the treated condition simply sees more data than the control. We audit the
overlap between the generated documents and the evaluation sets and find no
evaluation item that shares an eight-gram with any training derivation, on
ProofWriter, on FOLIO and on the BIG-Bench Hard subtasks of
Section~\ref{sec:bbh}.

\section{Experiments}

We fine-tune each base model on each mixture for one epoch with a global batch
of 128 sequences and a context window of 8{,}192 tokens, which follows from
measurement of the corpus, since the longest rendered derivation in the 25-hop
band occupies 7{,}700 tokens. A run aborts before it starts if more than 0.5
percent of its examples would be truncated. The main sweep trains Qwen2.5-7B
\citep{qwen2025qwen25} at shares of 1, 5, 10 and 25 percent in both renderings
with three random seeds per configuration. The same recipe, unchanged, then
trains Qwen2.5-3B and Qwen2.5-14B, Llama-3.1-8B, OLMo-2-7B and OLMoE-1B-7B,
which is a mixture of 64 experts with one billion active parameters, at the
five percent formal and ten percent English shares, with two seeds where the
control's own seed spread exceeds a point. Every model is a base model, since
the instruction tuning has to be ours and identical across conditions for the
claim about it to be testable. Base models that ship no chat template receive
a plain role-prefixed format that adds no vocabulary.
Appendix~\ref{sec:sweep-details} gives the remaining hyperparameters.

We evaluate on ProofWriter under the open-world assumption at inference depths
0 through 5, on FOLIO, on the deduction problems of the generator at chain
lengths 5 through 25, on GPQA-Diamond, on the three multi-hop question
answering datasets of LongBench, on BIG-Bench Hard, on HumanEval and MBPP, and
on six further general benchmarks. For the problems of the generator we prompt
the model in the document format on which it was trained, since a derivation
has to be requested before a checker can read it.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The procedure and the accuracy gain require different shares}

Table~\ref{tab:dose} reports the sweep on Qwen2.5-7B. The ability to write a
derivation that a checker accepts is already complete at one percent of the
mixture. A model trained without derivations writes none in six hundred
attempts, a model trained with a thousand of them writes a derivation in every
attempt, and a forward-chaining checker accepts more than nine in ten of those
derivations at chain length 25. The proofs run to 51 lines at that depth, and
the ability extends to chains of length 45, about twice the longest chain in
training, where the checker still accepts nine in ten. Below one percent the
behaviour is unreliable instead of merely weaker, since at 500 documents one seed
reaches 84.5 percent and another writes nothing.

Accuracy on held-out benchmarks requires a larger share. At one percent it
rises by about a point on ProofWriter at depth 3, which falls inside the spread
between seeds. At five percent it rises from 39.3 to 50.0 percent, and at ten
percent of English-rendered derivations it reaches 53.2 percent. Every seed of
every condition at five percent or more scores above every seed of the control,
and the spread among the three control seeds is 1.4 points. Accuracy on FOLIO
rises from 51.4 to 57.8 percent. Above five percent the curve is flat.

\subsection{Across families, scales and architectures}

Table~\ref{tab:family} repeats the treatment on five further base models. The
procedure transfers across the dense models. Every control below 14B writes
no accepted derivation at any chain length, and every treated dense arm except
OLMo-2 writes them on 96 to 100 percent of problems at every chain length,
including the arms whose accuracy did not improve. OLMo-2 reaches a quarter,
at chain length 5 as well as 25, so its shorter context does not explain the
gap. The mixture of experts does not acquire the procedure in either rendering,
at most 4.5 percent accepted at any depth from one seed. The
14B control reaches 7.5 percent at chain 25 by reasoning in prose instead of
in the trained format, and its treated arm reaches 99.

The accuracy effect is conditional. The English rendering raises ProofWriter
accuracy on every dense model, by 3.4 to 15.5 points, and it is the most stable arm
in the study, with the two Llama seeds agreeing to 0.8 points where that
model's control varies by 8.6. The formal rendering raises accuracy on Qwen at
all three scales and lowers it on OLMo-2 by 12.2 points, outside both seed
spreads. On the mixture of experts both renderings lower it, formal by 26.2
and English by 33.0, with the answer distribution collapsing onto a single
label in each case. Section~\ref{sec:prior}
explains why.

\subsection{General benchmarks}
\label{sec:bbh}

On Qwen2.5-7B the eight general benchmarks of Table~\ref{tab:general} in the
appendix stay within a few points of the control, and the three that leave the
control's seed range move upward. BIG-Bench Hard rises by 2.7 points, and
resolving it by subtask places the gain in the eight subtasks whose answer
follows by propagating a truth value or a state along a chain, which rise from
62.2 to 68.9 percent while the other nineteen move within a point. Web of lies,
the subtask closest in form to the generated data, rises from 85.5 to 97.6
percent, and reading the truth value the model carries at each position shows
the control losing it along the chain while the treated model does not, with
every condition answering correctly on essentially every item whose
intermediate values are all correct. Appendix~\ref{sec:wol-table} gives both
analyses.

This is a repaired deficit of one model, not a transfer effect. On Llama-3.1-8B
and Qwen2.5-3B the control already scores 99.8 and 99.6 on web of lies, no
chain subtask moves by more than 1.8 points, and the macro average moves by
less than one. GPQA-Diamond shows the same pattern on the 7B model, with its 73
items that ask for a computed value rising from 22.8 to 35.6 percent and its
125 recall items unchanged, and no movement elsewhere. Multi-hop question
answering gains at most three points on any model, and coding does not move on
any model. The formal rendering costs 11 points on long-context word counting,
which is aggregation and involves no propagation, and this cost is absent for the
English rendering.

\section{What the data supplies}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{An answer prior, and where it comes from}
\label{sec:prior}

ProofWriter presents a theory together with a claim and asks whether the claim
is true, false or unknown. Splitting the decidable items by the polarity of the
claim and by the gold label gives Table~\ref{tab:mechanism}. The Qwen2.5-7B
control answers correctly on 98.5 percent of the claims that are negated and
true and on 17.6 percent of the claims that are negated and false. It is not
failing to reason. It asserts that the claim holds, in three cases out of four
across the whole evaluation set, while the gold labels are close to balanced.
The derivations correct this. Accuracy on the negated and false claims rises to
56.9 percent, the probability of answering ``true'' falls from 0.73 to 0.50,
and the three remaining cells move by a few points at most. FOLIO shows the
same shape, with the false and uncertain items gaining and the true ones fixed,
and a log-likelihood probe in Appendix~\ref{sec:probe-table} shows the change
to be a removal of a margin offset on negated claims with a small
discrimination component.

\input{tab_mech_main}

The prior belongs to the instruction corpus, and Table~\ref{tab:corpus} shows
this by manipulation. We train the same base model with the same recipe on a
second instruction corpus drawn from Tulu 3, which has a different source and
task distribution. Its control answers ``false'' on 82 percent of items where
ours answers ``true'' on 73. The same five percent of derivations pulls both
distributions toward the labels, by 23 points of excess on one corpus and 12 on
the other, and accuracy rises on both. One of three treated seeds on the second
corpus did not learn the format and behaves as a control, so five percent sits
at the reliability threshold there where it is comfortably above it on ours.

\input{tab_corpus}

The same account covers the losses. Across the six models, accuracy moves with
how far the treatment pulls the answer distribution toward or away from the
label distribution. OLMo-2 starts well calibrated, with an excess of 8.6
points, and the formal derivations push it to 46 points of excess toward
``unknown'', so it loses ground. The mixture of experts starts with the highest
control accuracy in the study and the formal derivations remove ``false'' from
its answers entirely, while the English ones collapse it onto ``unknown''. The
English rendering moves every dense model toward the labels. A practitioner should therefore measure the model's answer distribution
against the benchmark's before choosing what to inject, and the English
rendering is the safer default.

\subsection{Order and depth}

Three manipulations on Qwen2.5-7B separate the procedure from the prior. The
first shuffles the lines of every training proof, which preserves every token,
the vocabulary, the length and the conclusion, and destroys only the order in
which the facts follow. On ProofWriter at depth 3 the ordered English
derivations gain 12.6 points over the control and the shuffled ones gain 7.8,
against 1.0 for length-matched ordinary documents, so three fifths of that
effect survives. On FOLIO almost none of it does, since the ordered arms gain
5.4 and 4.4 points and the shuffled arm gains 1.5. A shuffled derivation still
ends in a verdict and still carries negated conclusions, which is what the
prior needs, and no longer exhibits an inference, which is what FOLIO rewards.

The second varies the depth of the training proofs, and Table~\ref{tab:depth}
reports it. Trained on five-hop proofs the formal rendering writes accepted
derivations on every five-hop problem and on about a tenth of fifteen-hop
problems, where the band-25 arm holds above 99 percent everywhere. Benchmark
accuracy is indifferent to the band, sitting between 46 and 51 percent on
ProofWriter and 54 and 58 on FOLIO across every cell. The procedure needs
depth and the prior does not.

\input{tab_depth2}

The third removes the instruction data. Trained on derivations alone, in either
rendering, the model writes a derivation the checker accepts on every generated
problem and scores zero on every benchmark, because asked a plain question it
emits the opening token of the only format it knows and stops. The two
ingredients are each useless alone for this purpose. The derivations supply the
procedure and the instruction mixture supplies the ability to respond to an
arbitrary prompt.

\subsection{Chain-of-thought prompting}

If the improvement consisted of the model reasoning more, asking the control to
reason aloud would close the gap, and it does not. Under a prompt that requests
a chain of reasoning before the answer, the Qwen2.5-7B control reaches 39.9
percent on ProofWriter at depth 3, the formal conditions reach 45.3, 49.5 and
55.8 percent at five, ten and 25 percent of the mixture, and the English
conditions reach 44.1, 43.8 and 47.0. The advantage grows with the share in
exactly the regime where a well-prompted baseline is the natural objection.

\section{Checking the derivations}

A derivation written in a fixed format can be checked by a program. Our checker
reads the premises of an item and accepts a proof line when the line is a
premise or follows from lines already established, in either notation. On
ProofWriter it reproduces the published gold label on 99, 95, 91, 91 and 84
percent of items at depths 0 through 5, and its residual errors are answers of
``unknown'' on decided items, so it under-credits. On the generator's own
problems a derivation it accepts carries the correct answer in 99 percent of
samples, so selecting the first accepted derivation out of sixteen matches the
accuracy of asking whether any of them is correct, without reading a gold
answer. On ProofWriter an accepted derivation is correct between 53 and 69
percent of the time, because a third of the items ask whether a claim is
underivable and a forward chain never demonstrates that, and on GSM8K the
coupling nearly disappears, since a wrong answer there usually follows from
correct arithmetic inside a wrong plan. A checker pays where it is complete
with respect to the task.

\section{Notation}

The English rendering is the better default on three grounds. It raises
accuracy on every dense model measured, where the formal rendering raises it on
Qwen and lowers it on OLMo-2, and on the one sparse model both renderings fail
alike. It carries no measurable
cost on aggregation tasks, where the formal rendering costs 11 points on
long-context word counting. And it extrapolates beyond its training depth,
since trained on fifteen-hop proofs it writes accepted derivations on 99.5
percent of 25-hop problems where the formal rendering manages 45, and trained
on five-hop proofs it reaches 85.5 percent at fifteen hops where the formal
rendering collapses to 13.3. A controlled study on the generator alone,
reported in Appendix~\ref{sec:crossover}, finds formal supervision ahead at the
deepest training band when evaluation depth is held fixed, which answers a
different question. Performance at the training depth and extrapolation beyond
it have to be reported separately, and on the second the English rendering is
ahead at every band.

\section{Limitations}

Four further generators built to match specific target tasks, a program
variable carried through assignment and branching, membership in a reachable
set with half the questions unreachable, a position in a temporal order, and a
quantity carried through unit conversions, improve none of their targets when
mixed at five percent. Temporal sequences on BIG-Bench Hard falls 4 to 5 points
in both renderings where the deduction English arm raises it, HumanEval falls
5.2, and long-context variable tracking does not move. Matching a generator's
latent structure to a task is therefore not sufficient to improve that task,
and the extrapolation from our results to a general method for engineering
capabilities is not supported.

The accuracy gains beyond ProofWriter, on FOLIO, on the chain subtasks of
BIG-Bench Hard and on the quantitative half of GPQA-Diamond, are measured on
Qwen2.5-7B and do not replicate on the other families, whose controls are
already at ceiling on the subtasks concerned. The procedure is installed only
partially on OLMo-2, and it is absent on the mixture of experts, which has one
seed and whose sharded training ran several times slower than a dense model of
its active size, so a pathology specific to sparse routing under our trainer is
not excluded. Our checker is sound and incomplete, since it cannot
certify that a fact is underivable. The Tulu-trained models are verbose and do
not reliably stop after a short answer, so their accuracies are scored on
partly degenerate text, though their answer distributions are unambiguous and
the within-corpus comparison is sound.

\section{Conclusion}

A program that writes deduction problems and their solutions produces training
data with no teacher model in it, and a small quantity of it teaches an
instruction-tuned model two separable things. One is a procedure for writing
derivations a checker accepts, installed by one percent of the mixture, reaching
twice the training depth, and present on every dense model tested, in part on
OLMo-2, and absent on a mixture of experts. The other is a correction of the answer prior that the instruction
corpus installs, which we show by manipulating the corpus, and which helps
where the prior leans the wrong way and harms where it does not. Shuffled
proofs, shallow proofs and proofs without instruction data each keep one of the
two and lose the other. The English rendering carries the procedure further
and never worsens the prior on a dense model, and it is the rendering we
recommend.
