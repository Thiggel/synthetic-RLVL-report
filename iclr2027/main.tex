\begin{abstract}
Reasoning traces are usually treated as explanations of a solution, but they
also determine the token-level supervision from which a model learns. We study
whether formal derivations provide a useful complement to natural-language
reasoning traces. Our controlled experiments pair every prompt and answer with
two renderings of the same latent derivation. This design changes the trace
representation while holding the underlying problem fixed. On a repaired
unique-answer benchmark of branching deductions, formal supervision improves
out-of-distribution answer accuracy from $17.8\pm4.7$ to $75.0\pm9.5$ percent
at \passk{1} after training through depth 25. The corresponding correct and
valid rate rises from $17.5\pm4.5$ to $68.5\pm10.2$ percent. Formal traces also
improve answer accuracy on an independently generated attribute-constraint
task, although proof validity remains mixed. The gain is not universal.
Natural-language traces are stronger under shallower training, on a shortcut
control, and for one 32B single-modality comparison. A mode-conditioned 32B
model nevertheless improves its formal mode over formal-only training while
retaining a usable natural-language mode. These results show that the language
of intermediate reasoning changes what a model learns. They motivate a direct
transfer experiment that adds either matched formal or natural-language
derivations to continued language-model training and evaluates both conditions
after the same instruction-tuning stage.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Chain-of-thought supervision trains language models to produce intermediate
steps before answering a question~\citep{wei2022chain,kojima2022large}. Most
reasoning traces are written in natural language. This convention makes the
trace readable, but it also couples the computation to choices of phrasing,
discourse order, and repetition. A derivation can instead be written as a
sequence of explicit rule applications. Such a trace is less familiar to a
reader, yet it may provide a cleaner learning signal for the model.

This paper asks whether formal reasoning traces improve the reasoning learned
by a language model, including settings in which the model is not required to
use formal notation for every response. The question concerns training rather
than tool use. We do not call a theorem prover during inference, and we do not
replace language-model generation with symbolic execution. We change the
tokens used to supervise intermediate reasoning and test the resulting model
under matched generation budgets.

A direct comparison is difficult with ordinary corpora because two solutions
written in different styles may contain different computations. We address
this problem with paired synthetic data. A deterministic generator first
samples a latent derivation, then renders that derivation as either a compact
formal proof or a controlled natural-language explanation. The prompt, answer,
facts, rules, and proof graph are identical across the pair. The design allows
us to attribute a difference in learned behavior to the trace representation
rather than to problem selection or answer quality.

The controlled results reveal an interaction between representation and
reasoning depth. Natural-language supervision is stronger when the training
set contains only shallow and intermediate derivations. After the training
range reaches depth 25, formal supervision produces a large advantage on
longer proofs. On \branching{}, formal and natural-language models obtain
$75.0\pm9.5$ and $17.8\pm4.7$ percent out-of-distribution answer accuracy at
\passk{1}. An independent task, \attributes{}, also favors formal supervision
on answer correctness. These gains do not establish that formal traces are
always preferable. Natural language performs better in several controls, and
valid proof generation remains harder than answer prediction.

We next test whether both trace languages can coexist in one model. Joining
the two traces within one target leads to severe length-extrapolation failures.
Training separate examples with an output-mode tag is more promising. At 7B
parameters the conditioned model trails the corresponding single-modality
models. At 32B parameters its formal mode improves by 21.1 points over the
formal-only model at out-of-distribution \passk{1}, while its natural-language
mode remains below the natural-only model. This result suggests that formal
reasoning can be learned as an additional mode, but it does not show that
mixed supervision improves every mode equally.

The synthetic study provides the control needed to identify a trace effect.
The practical question is whether the effect transfers when formal traces form
a small part of ordinary language-model training. Our transfer experiment
therefore compares continued training on Dolmino against two matched mixtures.
One replaces 5 percent of the token stream with formal synthetic derivations,
and the other replaces the same fraction with natural-language renderings of
the same derivations. Each checkpoint receives the same instruction-tuning
stage before downstream evaluation. This three-way comparison asks whether any
gain comes from formal structure rather than from additional worked reasoning
problems.

Our contributions are as follows. We develop paired reasoning tasks that hold
the latent computation fixed while changing its textual representation. We
show that formal supervision can sharply improve long-depth answer accuracy,
but only after sufficient depth coverage, and we identify settings where the
effect does not hold. We separate answer accuracy, mechanical proof validity,
and sampled solution coverage throughout the evaluation. We also study two
ways of combining formal and natural-language supervision, which motivates a
matched transfer experiment in continued pretraining.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Reasoning traces.}
Chain-of-thought prompting and supervised rationales improve multi-step
reasoning in sufficiently large language models~\citep{wei2022chain,
kojima2022large}. Subsequent work constructs new rationales through
bootstrapping~\citep{zelikman2022star}, decomposes hard questions into smaller
ones~\citep{zhou2023least}, and searches over candidate reasoning
states~\citep{yao2023tree}. Scratchpad supervision similarly shows that an
autoregressive model can use generated intermediate state to solve algorithmic
tasks~\citep{nye2021show}. These studies establish the value of intermediate
computation. We study the representation in which that computation is taught.

\paragraph{Programs and symbolic representations.}
Program-aided language models and program-of-thought prompting use executable
code for intermediate computation~\citep{gao2023pal,chen2023program}.
Logic-based methods translate problems into symbolic forms and use an external
solver or verifier~\citep{pan2023logiclm,ling2023deductive}. Formal theorem
proving trains models to construct machine-checkable proofs in systems such as
Lean~\citep{polu2020generative,jiang2022draft,leandojo2023,
leanCopilot2024}. Our setting differs in two respects. The same model performs
all generation, and every formal target has a paired natural-language target
derived from the same proof. We can therefore measure the training effect of
the representation without changing the inference mechanism.

\paragraph{Faithfulness and verification.}
Natural-language rationales can omit factors that influence a prediction or
rationalize an answer after the fact~\citep{turpin2023language,
lanham2023measuring}. Formal syntax makes local correctness mechanically
testable, but it does not make a generated trace valid by construction. We
report answer accuracy and proof validity separately, and we retain sampled
coverage because a verifier can select among multiple generations without
access to the gold answer.

\paragraph{Controlled generalization.}
Synthetic tasks such as SCAN and COGS expose failures of systematic
generalization by controlling the train and test distributions
~\citep{lake2018generalization,keysers2020measuring}. We adopt the same use of
synthetic data as a measurement instrument. Our main shift increases proof
depth while preserving the rule system. We also train on spurious markers and
evaluate without them to test whether a trace representation changes shortcut
use~\citep{geirhos2020shortcut}.

\section{Paired Reasoning Experiments}
\label{sec:paired}

\subsection{Tasks and trace construction}

Each example begins with a latent directed acyclic proof. The generator emits
a prompt containing ground facts, rules, a query, and distractor branches. It
then traverses the supporting path to produce the target answer. A rendering
function writes the path in one of two trace languages. The formal rendering
uses atoms and explicit rule applications. The natural rendering expresses
the same substitutions and conclusions in controlled sentences. Both targets
end with the same answer field.

\branching{} tests composition across a chain of Horn-style inferences. Every
layer introduces a fresh constant. We compute the full Horn closure and reject
an example unless exactly one candidate answer is derivable. This closure test
is essential because an earlier version reused constants at long depths and
could derive several candidate answers. No result from that earlier generator
is used in this paper.

\attributes{} propagates values among object attributes under generated
constraints. Its vocabulary and proof construction differ from
\branching{}, which provides an independent test of the trace hypothesis. The
task is evaluated with its existing paired three-seed protocol.

Appendix~\ref{app:examples} gives complete paired targets. Appendix
~\ref{app:generator} describes the generators, uniqueness checks, distractor
construction, and validator interfaces.

\subsection{Models and training}

The main experiment fine-tunes OLMo-3-7B on 50,000 materialized examples per
condition. The maximum training depth is one of $5$, $10$, $15$, $20$, or
$25$. Every condition uses three seeds. We train LoRA adapters for 10,000
optimizer steps with rank 16, scaling 32, dropout 0.05, and learning rate
$10^{-4}$. The maximum training sequence length is 8192 tokens.

We evaluate at depths $\{1,2,5,10,12,15,18,20,25,30,35,40,45,50\}$. Each
depth contains 32 prompts. We draw 16 generations per prompt at temperature
1.0 and also evaluate greedy decoding. The context window is 16,384 tokens,
and both trace languages receive the same limit of 7,168 generated tokens. An
exhaustive tokenizer audit confirms that every gold prompt and target fits
within these limits.

We call depths above the maximum training depth out of distribution. For the
principal train-through-25 comparison, this set contains depths 30 through 50.
We report the probability that the first sampled generation is correct and
the probability that at least one of $k$ samples is correct. Joint accuracy
requires both the designated answer and a valid proof. Natural-language
validity is computed by translating the controlled trace into the same proof
checker, so we also report answer accuracy without this translation step.

Formal supervision produces a large advantage on the long-depth portion of
\branching{} once the training range reaches depth 25. The gain appears in
greedy decoding, \passk{1}, and the full sampling budget. It is not a choice
between answer accuracy and formal validity. Joint \passk{1} improves by 51.0
points, and joint \passk{16} improves by 57.9 points. The 98.1 percent joint
\passk{16} result also shows that almost every test prompt has at least one
correct proof in the model distribution.

The effect depends on the training range. Natural-language supervision is
stronger at greedy decoding and \passk{1} when the maximum training depth is
between 5 and 20. Formal supervision reverses the comparison only at depth 25.
A plausible explanation is that the model needs enough examples of deep
composition before the regularity of the formal language becomes useful. The
experiment identifies the interaction but does not by itself establish this
mechanism.

The attribute task provides a second positive result for answer correctness.
Formal supervision improves hard-depth accuracy by 8.8 points and depth-50
accuracy by 37.5 points. The latter estimate has high seed variance. Natural
language also has the higher hard-depth joint score. We therefore treat the
attribute result as supporting evidence for correctness, not as evidence that
formal supervision always improves valid proof generation.

\subsection{What changes the result}

We test whether the branching result follows from superficial properties of
the targets. Symbol padding lengthens formal atoms without changing the proof
grammar. It retains an out-of-distribution \passk{1} accuracy of
$67.3\pm17.5$ percent, compared with $75.0\pm9.5$ for compact formal traces.
Terse natural-language targets reach $64.1\pm24.9$ percent, although their
variance is high. A target-token-matched natural condition reaches
$41.0\pm20.1$ percent. These controls show that neither target length nor the
number of supervised tokens alone explains the main gap. They do not isolate
a single beneficial property of the formal trace.

The shortcut experiment does not support a general robustness claim. Models
are trained with a spurious schema marker on 80 percent of examples and tested
on marker-free examples. Natural-language supervision obtains
$78.5\pm14.9$ percent out-of-distribution \passk{1}, while formal supervision
obtains $43.1\pm7.7$ percent. This negative result prevents us from attributing
the depth gain to broad resistance against shortcuts.

Architecture controls are also mixed. Qwen2.5-7B is close at \passk{1}, with
$69.9\pm11.1$ for formal and $74.9\pm7.0$ for natural-language supervision.
In the single-modality OLMo-3-32B comparison, natural-language supervision is
much stronger at \passk{1}. The formal model nevertheless reaches
$98.8\pm0.9$ percent answer \passk{16}, compared with $99.8\pm0.3$ for the
natural model. The formal model therefore covers correct solutions but assigns
them less probability under unconstrained sampling.

\section{Combining Trace Languages}
\label{sec:combined}

Formal reasoning need not replace natural-language reasoning in every training
example or every generated response. We test two ways to combine the trace
languages. A same-example hybrid asks the model to generate both renderings in
one target. A conditioned model instead receives separate formal and natural
examples with an output-mode tag.

Both orders of the same-example hybrid fail on long proofs. The model often
copies a long prefix of one or both traces and reaches the generation limit
before producing an answer. Formal-then-natural targets obtain
$0.5\pm0.4$ percent out-of-distribution answer \passk{1}, and the reverse
order obtains $0.2\pm0.1$ percent. Combining the traces by concatenation makes
the sequence substantially harder without yielding a useful common model.

At 7B parameters, conditioned training trails both single-modality baselines.
The 32B result in Table~\ref{tab:conditioned} changes the interpretation. The
conditioned formal mode improves over formal-only training, which suggests
that the negative 7B result can reflect capacity or interference rather than a
failure of the output tags. The gain is mode specific. Natural-language
performance falls relative to the strong natural-only baseline. Raw
generations use the requested wrappers and show no meaningful mode leakage.
The remaining errors are incorrect branches and unfinished long proofs.

This experiment supports a limited claim. One sufficiently large model can
learn both trace languages, and exposure to natural-language derivations does
not prevent improvement in its formal mode. It does not show that conditioned
training dominates separate models. It instead motivates treating formal
traces as complementary data whose value should be measured at a natural
language interface.

\section{Transfer Through Continued Training}
\label{sec:transfer}

The paired experiments isolate a training effect on synthetic tasks. We now
test whether a small amount of formal reasoning data changes general language
model reasoning. The base model is Qwen2.5-7B. The ordinary training stream is
Dolmino, which was constructed for language-model midtraining rather than
generic web pretraining. We compare three token-matched conditions over five
billion tokens. The control uses Dolmino alone. The natural mixture replaces
5 percent of tokens with natural-language synthetic proofs. The formal mixture
replaces the same fraction with paired formal proofs.

The two injected datasets contain the same latent derivations and final
answers. All three streams use the same document wrappers, packing policy,
optimizer, learning-rate schedule, and token budget. We save checkpoints for
direct evaluation, then apply the same instruction-tuning data and selected
learning rate to every condition. The post-instruction comparison is primary
because the intended use of the model is ordinary question answering rather
than raw document continuation.

Our downstream suite includes mathematical reasoning, knowledge and
instruction benchmarks, and three context-provided multi-hop tasks. The
multi-hop evaluation covers HotpotQA, 2WikiMultiHopQA, and MuSiQue under the
same prompts and stopping rules. We preregister the unweighted macro across
the primary task set and the macro across the three multi-hop F1 scores. We
also report every task separately. A transfer claim requires the formal
mixture to exceed both the control and the matched natural mixture after the
same instruction tuning.

The terminal natural checkpoint and the matched instruction-tuning runs were
not complete when this draft was created. We therefore reserve the transfer
table rather than reporting an unmatched comparison. Preliminary direct
evaluation of the control and formal checkpoints is discussed only in
Appendix~\ref{app:preliminary}. It is not used to support the paper's main
claim.

\section{Discussion}

The experiments show that reasoning traces are not interchangeable labels for
the same computation. Their token structure changes optimization enough to
reverse generalization at greater proof depth. Formal supervision is most
useful in the regime where the model has seen sufficiently deep compositions,
and its high joint \passk{16} indicates that correct proofs are broadly
available in the sampled distribution. This latter result makes
verifier-guided selection and reinforcement learning natural follow-up
mechanisms. It does not imply that \passk{16} gains will automatically become
\passk{1} gains after posttraining.

The negative results narrow the conclusion. Formal traces do not improve every
task, architecture, or data perturbation. They can also produce the right
answer with an invalid proof. A formal-looking sequence should therefore not
be treated as evidence of faithful computation without mechanical checking.
The strongest supported conclusion is that formal derivations can be a better
source of supervision for some compositional regimes and can coexist with
natural-language behavior in a sufficiently large model.

The continued-training experiment tests the more consequential claim. If the
formal mixture improves downstream reasoning beyond both matched controls,
then formal derivations are useful even when the evaluated response is natural
language. If the three conditions remain tied after instruction tuning, the
paper still identifies where the synthetic advantage holds and where it fails
to transfer. The matched natural mixture is necessary in either case because
it separates formal structure from the value of adding worked solutions.

\section{Limitations}

The causal comparison relies on synthetic tasks. This control comes at the
cost of narrower semantics than open-domain reasoning. The large branching
gain appears only at the deepest training range and is not reproduced as a
\passk{1} advantage by every architecture. Natural-language validity also
depends on a task-specific translator, whereas formal validity is checked
directly. The 32B conditioned comparison changes both capacity and exposure
relative to the 7B study, so it does not identify which factor resolves the
earlier interference. Finally, the transfer experiment uses one base-model
family and a 5 percent mixture. Conclusions about broad midtraining practice
will require replication across models and mixture rates.

\section{Conclusion}

Formal derivations can provide a useful learning signal even when they are not
the model's only reasoning language. In paired synthetic experiments, formal
supervision yields a large long-depth gain after sufficient depth coverage and
improves answer correctness on a second constraint task. The same advantage is
not universal, and several controls favor natural-language traces. A
mode-conditioned 32B model shows that both trace languages can be learned by
one checkpoint while improving the weaker formal mode. The decisive practical
test is therefore not whether formal traces always replace natural language.
It is whether adding formal derivations improves ordinary downstream reasoning
more than adding matched natural-language solutions.

\section{Generator and Validation Details}
\label{app:generator}

For every branching example, the generator computes Horn closure over all
facts and rules before serialization. It accepts the example only if the
labeled answer is derivable and every competing candidate answer is absent
from the closure. Constants are unique across layers, which prevents unrelated
branches from merging at greater depths. Unit tests cover closure, paired
rendering, answer extraction, and both validity paths.

We will provide pseudocode for generation and the complete formal grammar in
the next draft. We will also report target-token distributions for every trace
ablation and the maximum prompt-plus-target length under the evaluation
tokenizer.

\section{Preliminary Transfer Diagnostics}
\label{app:preliminary}

Direct evaluation of the completed five-billion-token control and formal
checkpoints gives ten-task macros of 0.6246 and 0.6169. The formal checkpoint
improves all six measured cells across standard and tagged versions of
2WikiMultiHopQA, HotpotQA, and MuSiQue. Some generations exhibit stopping and
continuation artifacts, and the matched natural checkpoint was still running.
These observations motivate the common instruction-tuning stage but do not
yet provide a valid three-way transfer comparison.
