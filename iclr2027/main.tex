\title{The Effect of Program-Generated Deduction Data on
Instruction-Tuned Language Models}

\author{Anonymous authors}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Almost all synthetic training data in current practice is written by a
language model, which bounds the data by what the generating model knows and
propagates the errors that model makes. We study a source of training data
that contains no model at all. A program samples a multi-hop deduction
problem, solves it by forward chaining, and writes the solution out, so that
every document it produces is correct by construction. We replace part of an
ordinary instruction mixture with such derivations and we vary the replaced
share from one percent to twenty-five percent, while we hold the number of
training examples fixed at one hundred thousand and train three random seeds
for every configuration. We observe two effects that require different
doses of the generated data. One percent of the mixture, which amounts to one
thousand generated documents, takes Qwen2.5-7B from writing no derivation that a checker accepts
to writing derivations of fifty-one lines that a checker accepts in more than
nine cases out of ten, and the ability extends to chains about twice as long
as the longest chain seen in training. Accuracy on held-out deduction
benchmarks requires a larger dose and then stops improving. At five percent,
accuracy on ProofWriter at inference depth three rises from $39.3$ to $50.0$
percent, at ten percent of English-rendered derivations it reaches $53.2$
percent, and accuracy on FOLIO rises from $51.4$ to $57.8$ percent. Eight
general benchmarks move by less than the spread between seeds. We then ask what
the gain consists of and we find that instruction tuning leaves the model
answering affirmatively without regard to negation and that the synthetic
derivations repair this behaviour. The entire improvement lies in the claims
that are negated and false, where accuracy rises from $17.6$ to $56.9$
percent, the three remaining cells of the polarity by label square do not
move appreciably, and the probability that the model answers ``true'' falls
from $0.73$ to $0.50$ as the dose grows.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The public supply of human-written text is finite, and current projections
place its exhaustion for frontier training runs within this decade
\citep{villalobos2024position}. The standard remedy is synthetic text, and in
nearly every published recipe a language model writes that text. Textbook-style
corpora \citep{gunasekar2023textbooks, eldan2023tinystories}, rephrased web
documents \citep{maini2024rephrasing, maini2025beyondweb, su2025nemotroncc} and
prompted expansions of a small seed corpus \citep{yang2025synthetic} all take
this form. Data of this kind inherits the errors and the blind spots of the
generating model, and repeated training on such data narrows the distribution
of the trained model \citep{shumailov2024collapse, dohmatob2025strong}.
Verified synthetic data avoids the problem in principle
\citep{feng2025verification}, and individual systems have trained at scale on
program-generated derivations that a machine has checked
\citep{trinh2024alphageometry}.

A few hundred lines of Python are sufficient to sample a multi-hop deduction
problem, to solve it, and to write the solution out. The resulting documents
are correct by construction, they are available in unlimited quantity, and no
teacher model participates in producing them. The same documents are also
narrow and repetitive, they do not resemble text from the web, and they
describe a world of abstract constants that carry colour and direction
predicates and nothing else. We ask how much of such material an ordinary
instruction mixture needs before the model that results reasons measurably
better, and we ask what the resulting improvement consists of.

\input{fig_design}

We answer the first question with a dose sweep. We fine-tune Qwen2.5-7B
\citep{qwen2025qwen25} on one hundred thousand instruction examples and we
replace one, five, ten or twenty-five percent of those examples with
program-generated derivations, so that every condition sees the same number of
examples and the conditions differ only in the content of the replaced slice.
We repeat every configuration with three random seeds, and we render the
derivations in two notations, a formal one and a controlled English one, which
we draw from the same latent proofs. Figure~\ref{fig:overview} shows one
example of the generated data together with the two effects that the
replacement produces.

The two effects require different doses of the generated data. At one percent
of the mixture, which amounts to one thousand documents, the model acquires the ability to write a
derivation that a checker accepts. It writes proofs of fifty-one lines at chain
length twenty-five, the checker accepts more than nine in ten of those proofs,
and the ability extends to chains of length forty-five, which is about twice
the longest chain in training. A model trained on the same mixture without the
replacement writes no such derivation in six hundred attempts. At five percent
the model also becomes more accurate on held-out deduction benchmarks, and its
accuracy on ProofWriter \citep{tafjord2021proofwriter} at inference depth three
rises from $39.3$ to $50.0$ percent. Ten percent of English-rendered
derivations reaches $53.2$ percent and accuracy on FOLIO \citep{han2024folio}
rises by $6.4$ points. The curve is flat from five percent upward, so that a
quarter of the mixture buys nothing that a twentieth does not already provide.
Across eight general benchmarks, which include GSM8K, MMLU and ARC, the
conditions differ from the control by less than the spread between the seeds
of the unmodified control.

We answer the second question by locating the improvement within the
evaluation set. The instruction mixture on its own leaves the model with an
affirmative answer prior. The model answers that a claim holds in three cases
out of four, and it answers a claim and its negation identically in more than
a third of the items, and the synthetic derivations remove this behaviour. When
we split the held-out problems by the polarity of the claim and by the gold
label, we find the entire gain in one of the four resulting cells, the claims
that are negated and false, where accuracy rises from $17.6$ to $56.9$ percent
as the dose grows. The other three cells move by a few points at most, and the
probability of answering ``true'' falls monotonically with the dose from
$0.73$ to $0.50$. We therefore read the improvement as a correction of a
decision rule that instruction tuning installs, and we support this reading
with a length-matched control, a contamination audit, prior-invariant measures
of association, and paired significance tests.

Our contributions are the following.

\begin{itemize}
\item We measure how much program-generated deduction data an instruction
      mixture needs. One percent of the mixture installs the ability to write
      checkable derivations, five percent is sufficient for the full benchmark
      gain, and larger shares add nothing that we can measure.
\item We show that the gain is a repair of an affirmative answer prior left by
      instruction tuning, and we localise the gain to a single cell of a
      polarity by label split of the evaluation set.
\item We show that the derivations the model writes are machine-checkable, and
      we report both the setting in which a checker converts sampling into
      accuracy without an answer key and the setting in which it fails to do
      so.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Synthetic data written by models.} Most synthetic pretraining
corpora are produced by prompting a language model, either to write documents
in a chosen style \citep{gunasekar2023textbooks, eldan2023tinystories} or to
rewrite existing text from the web \citep{maini2024rephrasing,
maini2025beyondweb, su2025nemotroncc}. The quality of the result is bounded by
the generating model, and training repeatedly on such text degrades the
distribution of the trained model \citep{shumailov2024collapse,
dohmatob2025strong}. The source we study contains no model, so the capability
of the generator is the capability of the program that writes the data.

\paragraph{Synthetic deduction corpora.} Several corpora present deduction
problems to language models for training or for evaluation, among them
RuleTaker and ProofWriter \citep{clark2020transformers, tafjord2021proofwriter},
PrOntoQA \citep{saparov2023language} and FLD \citep{morishita2023fld,
morishita2024alt}. These corpora render their proofs in English.
\citet{zhou2025finelogic} compare symbolic supervision with English
supervision on matched problems and report that English supervision
generalises further. We generate both renderings from a single latent proof,
which allows us to hold the problem and the answer fixed while we vary only
the notation, and we find that the ordering of the two notations depends on
the depth of the training proofs.

\paragraph{Verifiable rewards and checkable traces.} Training a model against
a verifier has become a standard recipe in mathematics and in code
\citep{lambert2025tulu, yue2025does}, and formal systems allow a machine to
check a proof \citep{trinh2024alphageometry}. The derivations we generate are
checkable by a small forward-chaining program, which allows us to ask whether
a checker can select a correct sample without an answer key, and to measure
how far that selection carries to a benchmark that people wrote.

\paragraph{Answer priors in tuned models.} Instruction-tuned models are known
to acquire response biases that are separable from their underlying competence
\citep{ouyang2022training}, and negation in particular remains difficult for
language models \citep{kassner2020negated, hosseini2021understanding,
truong2023language}. Our mechanistic result connects these two lines of work.
The pathology that synthetic deduction data repairs is an affirmative answer
prior that instruction tuning installs, and the repair appears as a change in
that prior while accuracy on the remaining items stays where it was.

\section{The generator and the training mixtures}

The generator samples a set of constants, a set of unary predicates and a set
of implication rules, chooses a target chain length, and constructs a problem
whose answer follows from the premises by that many applications of modus
ponens. It then solves the problem by forward chaining and writes the solution
out. No language model participates in any step of the pipeline, and every
document is correct by construction. We restrict the vocabulary of the
generator deliberately, and it names its constants $c_0$ through $c_{25}$ and draws its
predicates from a list of colour and direction words, so that the documents
carry no world knowledge and share almost no vocabulary with the benchmarks on
which we later evaluate.

The generator can write each latent proof in two ways.
Figure~\ref{fig:overview-a} shows both renderings of one sampled problem. The
formal rendering declares the constants and the predicate symbols, states the
premises as implications between atoms, and lists the proof as numbered lines
together with the rule that justifies each line. The English rendering states
the same premises and the same proof as ordinary sentences. The two renderings
share the prompt, the answer and the proof graph, so that a comparison between
them isolates the effect of the notation.

We build every training mixture by replacement. A condition with a share $p$
contains $(1-p)\cdot 10^5$ examples of the instruction corpus and $p \cdot
10^5$ rendered derivations, so that all conditions train on exactly one
hundred thousand examples and differ only in the content of the replaced
slice. We draw the evaluation split of every mixture from the instruction
corpus alone. This design removes the most common confound in experiments on
data augmentation, which is that the treated condition simply sees more data
than the control.

We audit the overlap between the generated documents and the evaluation sets.
No evaluation item shares an eight-gram with any training derivation, and the
two hundred most frequent words of the two corpora overlap in four words. The
words on which the benchmarks rely, among them ``not'', ``someone'' and the
animal nouns of ProofWriter, never occur in the training derivations.

\section{The effect of the replacement share}

\subsection{Experimental setup}

We fine-tune Qwen2.5-7B on each mixture for one epoch with a learning rate of
$5\times 10^{-6}$, a global batch of one hundred and twenty-eight sequences
and a context window of eight thousand one hundred and ninety-two tokens, which
we set from measurement of the corpus. The longest rendered derivation in the
twenty-five hop band occupies seven thousand seven hundred tokens, and we make
truncation fail-closed, so that a run aborts if more than half a percent of
its examples would be cut. We train three seeds for every configuration and we
report all of them.

We evaluate on ProofWriter under the open-world assumption at inference depths
zero through five, on FOLIO, on the deduction problems of the generator at
chain lengths five through twenty-five, and on eight general benchmarks. For
the problems of the generator we prompt the model in the document format on
which it was trained, because a derivation has to be requested before a
checker can read it.

\subsection{Results}

Table~\ref{tab:dose} reports the sweep, and the two effects we describe below
separate cleanly.

The first effect is the ability to write a derivation that a checker accepts,
and this ability is already complete at one percent of the mixture. A model
trained without derivations writes none in six hundred attempts. A model
trained with one thousand of them writes a derivation in every attempt, and a
forward-chaining checker accepts more than nine in ten of those derivations at
chain length twenty-five. The proofs run to fifty-one lines at that depth,
which corresponds to two lines for every hop together with the premises, and
the ability extends to chains of length forty-five, about twice the longest
chain in training, where the checker still accepts nine in ten.

The second effect is accuracy on held-out benchmarks, and it requires a larger
dose. At one percent, accuracy on ProofWriter at depth three rises by about a
point, which falls within the spread between seeds. At five percent it rises
from $39.3$ to $50.0$ percent, and at ten percent of English-rendered
derivations it reaches $53.2$ percent. Every seed of every condition at five
percent or more scores above every seed of the control, and the spread among
the three control seeds is $1.4$ points. Accuracy on FOLIO rises from $51.4$
to $57.8$ percent. Beyond five percent the curve is flat, and a condition with
a quarter of the mixture replaced scores $51.6$ percent, which lies within the
noise of the five percent condition.

We cannot measure a cost of the intervention in our evaluation. Across GSM8K,
BBH, MMLU, ARC-Challenge, LogiQA, HellaSwag, PIQA and WinoGrande, no condition
differs from the control by more than the spread between the control seeds.

\section{The source of the improvement}

\subsection{Accuracy by claim polarity and gold label}

ProofWriter presents a theory together with a claim and asks whether the claim
is true, false or unknown. We split the evaluation items by two properties
that the model can read off the prompt, which are whether the claim is negated
and what the gold label is, and we measure accuracy in each of the four
resulting cells for the decidable items. Table~\ref{tab:mechanism} reports the
split.

The control answers correctly on $98.5$ percent of the claims that are negated
and true, on $68$ percent of the claims that are positive and false, and on
$75$ percent of the claims that are positive and true. On the claims that are
negated and false it answers correctly on $17.6$ percent. The pattern is not
the pattern of a model that fails to reason. The control answers that the
claim holds, and it does so in three cases out of four across the whole
evaluation set, while the gold labels are close to balanced.

The synthetic derivations correct this behaviour, and as the dose grows accuracy
on the negated and false claims rises from $17.6$ to $56.9$ percent, the
probability of answering ``true'' falls monotonically from $0.73$ to $0.50$,
and the three remaining cells move by a few points at most. The large cell of
positive and true claims drifts downward by about eight points, which is the
direction that a correction of an affirmative prior predicts. We therefore
read the improvement as a change in a decision rule, and the split makes that
change directly visible.

\subsection{Control conditions}

We run three controls in order to bound what else could produce the effect.

The first control concerns length and domain. In a midtraining study on the
same generator we replaced the same share of the corpus with ordinary
documents drawn from the pretraining mixture and matched to the derivations in
length distribution. That condition is indistinguishable from the control on
ProofWriter, with a paired test giving $p=0.62$ and $p=1.00$ in two seeds,
while the derivation condition gains $12.6$ points. Length and domain
therefore do not explain the gain.

The second control concerns memorisation. No evaluation item shares an
eight-gram with any training derivation, and the vocabulary overlap is four
words among the two hundred most frequent.

The third control concerns the role of deductive order. We midtrain a further
condition on the same derivations with the lines of each proof shuffled, which
preserves every token, the vocabulary, the length and the conclusion, and
destroys only the order in which the facts follow from one another. This
condition is in training at the time of writing and we will report it with the
same measures.

\subsection{Comparison with chain-of-thought prompting}

If the improvement consisted simply of the model reasoning more, then asking the control to reason aloud should close the gap, and it does not.
Under a prompt that requests a chain of reasoning before the answer, the
control reaches $39.9$ percent on ProofWriter at inference depth three, the
five percent condition reaches $45.3$ percent and the ten percent condition
reaches $49.4$ percent. A condition with a quarter of the mixture replaced
reaches $55.8$ percent, so that the advantage grows with the dose in exactly
the regime where a well-prompted baseline is the natural objection.

\section{Mechanical checking of the written derivations}

A derivation written in a fixed format can be checked by a program. We
implement a forward-chaining checker that reads the premises of an item and
accepts a proof line when the line is a premise or follows from lines that are
already established. The checker reads both notations, and it translates
formal lines through the predicate legend that the model itself writes. On
ProofWriter it reproduces the published gold label on $99$, $95$, $91$, $91$
and $84$ percent of the items at inference depths zero through five, and its
residual errors are answers of ``unknown'' on items whose gold label is
decided, so that the checker under-credits the model.

On the problems of the generator the checker behaves almost as an oracle. A
derivation that the checker accepts carries the correct answer in $99$ percent
of samples, and selecting the first accepted derivation out of sixteen samples
gives the same accuracy as asking whether any of the sixteen is correct. A
selection rule that uses no gold answer therefore matches sixteen-sample
coverage.

On ProofWriter the same rule is much weaker. When we sample sixteen
derivations per item at temperature $0.8$, between $28$ and $37$ percent of
the items carry a proof that the checker accepts, against none for the
control, and the accuracy of an accepted derivation lies between $53$ and $69$
percent. Selection by the checker therefore trails majority voting over the
same samples. We attribute the difference to the structure of the task. The
checker certifies that the written steps follow from the premises, and
ProofWriter asks in a third of its items whether a claim is underivable, which
a forward chain never demonstrates. A checker becomes useful once it is
complete with respect to the task, and soundness on its own is insufficient.

\section{Comparison of the two notations}

The dose sweep shows the two notations transferring about equally, with the
English rendering ahead at ten percent and the formal rendering ahead at five.
A controlled study on the generator alone shows that the ordering is not fixed
and that the depth of the training proofs decides it. When we fine-tune
OLMo-3-7B on fifty thousand paired examples per condition, English supervision
generalises further as long as the training proofs reach at most twenty hops.
At twenty-five hops the ordering reverses, and formal supervision reaches
$75.0\pm9.5$ percent out-of-distribution accuracy against $17.8\pm4.7$ percent
for English supervision. Sixteen samples move the reversal to fifteen hops, so
that formal supervision first widens the set of problems the model can reach
and only later improves its first attempt.
Appendix~\ref{sec:crossover-figure} reports the full curves.

The two settings agree once we state the regime in which each of them holds.
The advantage of the formal rendering appears under supervision that is both
dense and deep, and an instruction mixture with a tenth of its examples
replaced is neither dense nor deep in that sense. We therefore do not
recommend the formal rendering on the strength of the controlled study, and we
report the two notations as interchangeable at the doses a practitioner would
plausibly use.

\section{Midtraining as an alternative point of insertion}

The same derivations can enter during midtraining. We midtrained Qwen2.5-7B on
2.5 billion tokens with a tenth of the mixture replaced, and we then applied
the same instruction tuning to every condition. That route also produces the
gain on ProofWriter, which amounts to $12.6$ points for the English rendering
at inference depth three with two instruction-tuning seeds, and it also leaves
the general benchmarks flat, with the exception of a loss of two to three
points on LogiQA that replicates across seeds.

When we compare the two routes, midtraining is the more expensive one and buys
no more. The instruction-tuning route reaches the same accuracy, it
generalises further on long chains, and it does not carry the cost on LogiQA.
Applying both routes in sequence adds nothing to either one, and on the
problems of the generator the model trained in both stages performs as the
midtrained model alone performs. The midtrained model retains one ability that
instruction tuning without derivations removes completely. Prompted in the
format of its training documents, the midtrained model writes checkable proofs
up to chain length twenty-five, and after ordinary instruction tuning it
writes none.

\section{Limitations}

The gains we measure are confined to deduction. ProofWriter and FOLIO improve,
the eight general benchmarks do not move, and we have no evidence that the
intervention helps a task that does not ask for a chain of inferences.

The mechanism we identify is a correction of an answer prior. The effect is
real and it replicates, and the claim it supports is narrower than a claim
about an improvement in reasoning capability. A model whose prior is already
balanced may gain less than the model we study.

Our checker is sound and it is incomplete. It certifies that written steps
follow from the premises, and it cannot certify that a fact is underivable,
which limits the benefit of checker-based selection on benchmarks whose
answers are open-world.

We train one model family at one scale. The affirmative prior that the
derivations repair is a property of the instruction corpus we use, and a
different instruction corpus may leave a different pathology behind.

\section{Conclusion}

A program that writes deduction problems together with their solutions
produces training data that contains no teacher model, and a small amount of
that data changes what an instruction-tuned language model does. One percent
of an ordinary mixture is sufficient for the model to write proofs that a
checker accepts, at chain lengths about twice those it saw in training, where
a model trained without the replacement writes none. Five percent is
sufficient for the full improvement on held-out deduction benchmarks, and
larger shares add nothing that we can measure. We read the improvement as a
correction of an affirmative answer prior that instruction tuning installs,
the improvement is confined to the claims on which that prior does damage, and
it costs nothing on the general benchmarks we measure.
