\title{Garbage In, Performance Out: The Surprising Effect of Symbolically
Generated Deduction Data on LLMs}

\author{Anonymous authors}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Synthetic training data for language models is almost always written by
another language model, and its quality is bounded by the model that writes
it. We study the opposite extreme, a corpus of multi-hop deduction problems
and their solutions that a short program generates without any model in the
loop. The documents are correct by construction, but they carry no world
knowledge, use abstract constant names and apply a single inference rule
throughout. We replace a small fraction of a standard instruction-tuning
mixture with such documents while keeping the number of training examples
fixed, and we find that they improve the resulting model substantially. On
Qwen2.5-7B, replacing 5 to 10 percent of the mixture raises accuracy by 14
points on ProofWriter, 6 points on FOLIO, 7 points on the multi-step subtasks
of BIG-Bench Hard and 13 points on the quantitative items of GPQA-Diamond,
without degrading general benchmarks. As little as 1 percent of the mixture
teaches the model to write long derivations that a symbolic checker accepts,
an ability that instruction tuning alone does not produce in any of the five
model families we study. We identify two mechanisms behind these gains. The
generated data teaches a procedure for carrying an intermediate result
through a chain of steps, and it corrects a systematic bias in the answers of
instruction-tuned models that we trace to the instruction corpus itself. Both
mechanisms transfer across model families and scales, and together they
predict when the data helps and when it does not.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Synthetic data has become a standard ingredient in the training of language
models. Textbook-style corpora \citep{gunasekar2023textbooks,
eldan2023tinystories}, rephrased web documents \citep{maini2024rephrasing,
maini2025beyondweb, su2025nemotroncc} and prompted expansions of a seed corpus
\citep{yang2025synthetic} have all been shown to improve downstream
performance. In nearly every case, however, the synthetic text is written by
another language model. Its quality is therefore bounded by the knowledge and
the reasoning of the model that writes it, and repeated training on such text
has been shown to narrow the distribution of the trained model
\citep{shumailov2024collapse, dohmatob2025strong}.

An alternative source of synthetic data is a program. A generator of a few
hundred lines can sample a multi-hop deduction problem, solve it by forward
chaining and write the solution out, which yields documents that are correct
by construction and unlimited in number. Corpora of this kind have been used
to evaluate the reasoning of language models \citep{clark2020transformers,
tafjord2021proofwriter, saparov2023language} and to train models on deduction
in isolation \citep{morishita2023fld, morishita2024alt}. Whether they are
useful as an ingredient of general instruction tuning is much less clear. The
documents contain no world knowledge, they describe abstract constants with
arbitrary attributes, and they apply the same inference rule for dozens of
lines, so there is little reason to expect them to improve a model on
anything beyond the generator's own problems.

In this work we show that such data does improve a general instruction-tuned
model by a substantial margin, and we investigate why.
We fine-tune base models on a fixed instruction mixture of 100{,}000 examples
in which a fraction of the instruction examples is replaced by generated
derivations, so that every condition trains on the same number of examples
and the treatment adds no data. On Qwen2.5-7B \citep{qwen2025qwen25},
replacing 5 to 10 percent of the mixture improves accuracy on ProofWriter
\citep{tafjord2021proofwriter} by 13.9 points, on FOLIO \citep{han2024folio}
by 6.4 points, on the multi-step subtasks of BIG-Bench Hard by 6.7 points and
on the quantitative items of GPQA-Diamond by 12.8 points, while eight general
benchmarks are unchanged. Replacing only 1 percent of the mixture, which
amounts to 1{,}000 documents, is enough for the model to write derivations of
more than 50 lines that a symbolic checker accepts, at twice the chain length
seen in training. Instruction tuning alone does not produce this ability in
any of the five model families we study, and the treatment installs it on
every dense model among them.

The second part of the paper asks how documents with no content can have this
effect. We find that the generated data supplies two things that the
instruction corpus does not, and a series of controlled manipulations
separates them. The first is a procedure for carrying an intermediate value
through a chain of steps. Shuffling the lines of the training proofs removes
it, the depth of the training proofs bounds it, and on the multi-step subtasks
of BIG-Bench Hard we observe the untreated model losing the carried value
midway through a chain where the treated model retains it. The second is a
correction of a systematic bias in the answers of the instruction-tuned model.
On ProofWriter the untreated model asserts that a claim holds in three cases
out of four, and the entire accuracy gain comes from the negated claims that
are false. We show that the instruction corpus installs this bias, since
training the same base model on a second instruction corpus installs the
opposite bias and the same derivations correct it in the opposite direction.
Across nine treated models from three families, the change in accuracy is
predicted by how far the treatment moves the model's answer distribution
toward the label distribution, which also accounts for the two models on
which the formal rendering of the derivations lowers accuracy.

Our contributions are as follows. (i)~We show that a small fraction of
program-generated deduction data, substituted into an ordinary instruction
mixture, improves an instruction-tuned model on deduction and multi-step
benchmarks well beyond the generator's domain (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
(ii)~We show that the data installs a derivation-writing procedure across four
dense model families at 1 percent of the mixture, and we characterise the
models on which it fails (Section~\ref{sec:families}). (iii)~We identify the
two mechanisms responsible for the accuracy gains and separate them by
manipulating the order, the depth and the presence of the training proofs
and by changing the instruction corpus (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}). (iv)~We
compare a formal and an English rendering of the same latent proofs and find
that the English rendering generalises further in depth and improves accuracy
on every dense model, where the formal rendering helps some models and harms
others (Section~\ref{sec:notation}).

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Synthetic data written by models.} Most synthetic pretraining
corpora are produced by prompting a language model, either to write documents
in a chosen style \citep{gunasekar2023textbooks, eldan2023tinystories} or to
rewrite existing text from the web \citep{maini2024rephrasing,
maini2025beyondweb, su2025nemotroncc}. The quality of the result is bounded by
the generating model, and repeated training on such text degrades the
distribution of the trained model \citep{shumailov2024collapse,
dohmatob2025strong}. The data we study is generated by a program and involves
no model at any stage.

\paragraph{Synthetic deduction corpora.} Several corpora present deduction
problems to language models for training or for evaluation, among them
RuleTaker and ProofWriter \citep{clark2020transformers, tafjord2021proofwriter},
PrOntoQA \citep{saparov2023language} and FLD \citep{morishita2023fld,
morishita2024alt}. These corpora render their proofs in English.
\citet{zhou2025finelogic} compare symbolic supervision with English supervision
on matched problems and report that English supervision generalises further.
In our setting both renderings are produced from a single latent proof, which
allows a direct comparison, and we reach a similar conclusion for
generalisation in depth and across model families.

\paragraph{Verifiable rewards and checkable traces.} Training a model against
a verifier has become a standard recipe in mathematics and in code
\citep{lambert2025tulu, yue2025does}, and formal systems allow a machine to
check a proof \citep{trinh2024alphageometry}. The derivations studied here are
checked by a small forward-chaining program, and we discuss the relation to
verifier-based training in Section~\ref{sec:substrate}.

\paragraph{Answer biases in tuned models.} Instruction-tuned models acquire
response biases that are separable from their underlying competence
\citep{ouyang2022training}, and negation in particular remains difficult for
language models \citep{kassner2020negated, hosseini2021understanding,
truong2023language}. We locate one such bias, attribute it to the instruction
corpus by manipulation, and show which training data corrects it.

\section{Generated data}
\label{sec:data}

The generator samples a set of constants, a set of unary predicates and a set
of implication rules, chooses a target chain length, and constructs a problem
whose answer follows from the premises by that many applications of modus
ponens. It then solves the problem by forward chaining and writes the solution
out, so that every document is correct by construction and no language model
participates in any step. The vocabulary is kept deliberately small.
Constants are named $c_0$ through $c_{25}$ and predicates are drawn from a
list of colour and direction words, so that the documents carry no world
knowledge. Figure~\ref{fig:overview-a} shows one generated example.

\input{fig_design}

Each latent proof admits two renderings. The formal rendering declares the
constants and the predicate symbols, states the premises as implications
between atoms, and lists the proof as numbered lines together with the rule
that justifies each line. The English rendering states the same premises and
the same proof as ordinary sentences. The prompt, the answer and the proof
graph are common to the two renderings, so a comparison between them isolates
the effect of notation.

Every training mixture is built by replacement. A condition with share $p$
contains $(1-p)\cdot 10^5$ examples of the instruction corpus and $p \cdot
10^5$ rendered derivations, so that all conditions train on exactly $10^5$
examples and differ only in the content of the replaced slice. The evaluation
split of every mixture is drawn from the instruction corpus alone. This design
avoids the most common confound in experiments on data augmentation, namely
that the treated condition sees more data than the control. We audited the
overlap between the generated documents and the evaluation sets and found no
evaluation item on ProofWriter, FOLIO or the BIG-Bench Hard subtasks of
Section~\ref{sec:bbh} that shares an eight-gram with any training derivation.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

We fine-tune each base model on each mixture for one epoch with a global batch
of 128 sequences and a context window of 8{,}192 tokens. The window follows
from the corpus, since the longest rendered derivation at chain length 25
occupies 7{,}700 tokens, and a run is aborted before it starts if more than
0.5 percent of its examples would be truncated. The main sweep trains
Qwen2.5-7B at shares of 1, 5, 10 and 25 percent in both renderings with three
random seeds per configuration. The same recipe then trains Qwen2.5-3B and
Qwen2.5-14B, Llama-3.1-8B, OLMo-2-7B and OLMoE-1B-7B, a mixture of 64 experts
with one billion active parameters, at the 5 percent formal and 10 percent
English shares, with two seeds where the seed spread of the control exceeds
one point. All models are base models, since the instruction tuning has to be
ours and identical across conditions for the claims about it to be testable.
Base models that ship no chat template receive a plain role-prefixed format
that adds no vocabulary. Appendix~\ref{sec:sweep-details} gives the remaining
hyperparameters.

We evaluate on ProofWriter under the open-world assumption at inference depths
0 through 5, on FOLIO, on the deduction problems of the generator at chain
lengths 5 through 45, on GPQA-Diamond, on the three multi-hop question
answering datasets of LongBench, on BIG-Bench Hard, on HumanEval and MBPP, and
on six further general benchmarks. For the problems of the generator we prompt
the model in the document format on which it was trained, since a derivation
has to be requested before a checker can read it. Unless stated otherwise,
we report the mean over seeds.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Effect of the replaced share}

Table~\ref{tab:dose} reports the sweep on Qwen2.5-7B. The ability to write a
derivation that the checker accepts is already complete at 1 percent of the
mixture. A model trained without derivations writes none in 600 attempts,
whereas a model trained with 1{,}000 of them writes a derivation in every
attempt, and the checker accepts more than nine in ten of those derivations at
chain length 25. The proofs run to 51 lines at that depth, and the ability
extends to chains of length 45, about twice the longest chain in training,
where the checker still accepts nine in ten. Below 1 percent the behaviour
becomes unreliable, since at 500 documents one seed reaches 84.5 percent
acceptance and another writes no derivation at all.

Accuracy on held-out benchmarks requires a larger share. At 1 percent it
rises by about one point on ProofWriter at depth 3, which is within the seed
spread. At 5 percent it rises from 39.3 to 50.0 percent, and at 10 percent of
English-rendered derivations it reaches 53.2 percent. Every seed of every
condition at 5 percent or more scores above every seed of the control, whose
three seeds span 1.4 points. Accuracy on FOLIO rises from 51.4 to 57.8
percent. Above 5 percent the curve is flat.
Figure~\ref{fig:overview-b} summarises the gains at the 10 percent English
share.

\subsection{Model families, scales and architectures}
\label{sec:families}

Table~\ref{tab:family} repeats the treatment on five further base models. The
derivation procedure transfers across the dense models. Every control below
14B parameters writes no accepted derivation at any chain length, and every
treated dense model except OLMo-2 writes them on 96 to 100 percent of problems
at every chain length, including the models whose accuracy did not improve.
OLMo-2 reaches about a quarter, at chain length 5 as well as 25, so its shorter
context does not explain the gap. The mixture of experts does not acquire the
procedure in either rendering, with at most 4.5 percent accepted at any depth
from one seed. The 14B control reaches 7.5 percent at chain length 25 by
reasoning in prose, and its treated counterpart reaches 99 percent.

The accuracy effect depends on the rendering and on the model. The English
rendering raises ProofWriter accuracy on every dense model, by 3.4 to 15.5
points, and it is the most stable condition in the study, with the two Llama
seeds agreeing to 0.8 points where the Llama control varies by 8.6. The formal
rendering raises accuracy on Qwen at all three scales and lowers it on OLMo-2
by 12.2 points, which is outside both seed spreads. On the mixture of experts
both renderings lower accuracy, the formal one by 26.2 points and the English
one by 33.0, and in each case the answer distribution collapses onto a single
label. Section~\ref{sec:prior} explains these losses.

\subsection{General benchmarks}
\label{sec:bbh}

On Qwen2.5-7B the eight general benchmarks of Table~\ref{tab:general} in the
appendix stay within a few points of the control, and the three that move
beyond the seed spread move upward. BIG-Bench Hard rises by 2.7 points, and
resolving it by subtask places the gain in the eight subtasks whose answer is
obtained by propagating a truth value or a state along a chain. These rise
from 62.2 to 68.9 percent while the other nineteen move within one point. Web
of lies, the subtask closest in form to the generated data, rises from 85.5 to
97.6 percent. GPQA-Diamond shows a similar pattern, since the 73 items that
ask for a computed value rise from 22.8 to 35.6 percent while the 125 items
that ask for a fact are unchanged. Multi-hop question answering gains between
one and three points of F1, and HumanEval and MBPP do not move. The formal
rendering costs 11 points on a long-context task that counts common words,
which involves aggregation and no propagation, and this cost is absent for
the English rendering.

The gains on BIG-Bench Hard and GPQA-Diamond are specific to the 7B model. On
Llama-3.1-8B and Qwen2.5-3B the control already scores 99.8 and 99.6 on web of
lies, no chain subtask moves by more than 1.8 points, and the macro average
moves by less than one. We therefore interpret these gains as the repair of a
deficit that the 7B model happens to have, and we return to what that deficit
is in Section~\ref{sec:procedure}.

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{A bias in the answer distribution, and where it comes from}
\label{sec:prior}

ProofWriter presents a theory together with a claim and asks whether the claim
is true, false or unknown. Splitting the decidable items by the polarity of the
claim and by the gold label gives Table~\ref{tab:mechanism}. The Qwen2.5-7B
control answers correctly on 98.5 percent of the claims that are negated and
true and on 17.6 percent of the claims that are negated and false. It asserts
that the claim holds in three cases out of four across the whole evaluation
set, while the gold labels are close to balanced. The derivations correct
this. Accuracy on the negated and false claims rises to 56.9 percent, the
probability of answering ``true'' falls from 0.73 to 0.50, and the three
remaining cells move by a few points at most. FOLIO shows the same shape, with
the false and uncertain items gaining and the true ones fixed, and a
log-likelihood probe in Appendix~\ref{sec:probe-table} shows the change to be
mostly a shift of the decision margin on negated claims with a small
discrimination component.

\input{tab_mech_main}

Table~\ref{tab:corpus} shows by manipulation that the bias comes from the
instruction corpus. We train the same base model with the same recipe on a
second instruction corpus drawn from Tulu 3, which has a different source and
task distribution. Its control answers ``false'' on 82 percent of items where
ours answers ``true'' on 73. The same 5 percent of derivations pulls both
distributions toward the labels, by 23 points of excess on one corpus and 12
on the other, and accuracy rises on both. One of three treated seeds on the
second corpus did not learn the format and behaves as a control, so 5 percent
sits at the reliability threshold there where it is comfortably above it on
ours.

\input{tab_corpus}

The same account covers the losses of Section~\ref{sec:families}. Across the
nine treated models from three families, accuracy moves with how far the
treatment pulls the answer distribution toward or away from the label
distribution. OLMo-2 starts well calibrated, with an excess of 8.6 points, and
the formal derivations push it to 46 points of excess toward ``unknown'', so
it loses accuracy. The mixture of experts starts with the highest control
accuracy in the study, and the formal derivations remove ``false'' from its
answers entirely while the English ones collapse it onto ``unknown''. The
English rendering moves every dense model toward the labels. Since the excess
of the control's answer distribution over the label distribution can be
measured before any training, and since the sign of the accuracy change agrees
with the sign of the change in that excess in every one of the nine models,
the diagnostic can be used to decide whether and which rendering to inject
before a model is trained.

\subsection{A procedure for carrying a value through a chain}
\label{sec:procedure}

Three manipulations on Qwen2.5-7B separate the procedure from the correction
of the bias. The first shuffles the lines of every training proof, which
preserves every token, the vocabulary, the length and the conclusion, and
destroys only the order in which the facts follow. On ProofWriter at depth 3
the ordered English derivations gain 12.6 points over the control and the
shuffled ones gain 7.8, against 1.0 for length-matched ordinary documents, so
about three fifths of the effect survives. On FOLIO almost none of it does,
since the ordered conditions gain 5.4 and 4.4 points and the shuffled one gains
1.5. A shuffled derivation still ends in a verdict and still carries negated
conclusions, which is what the correction of the bias requires, but it no
longer exhibits an inference, which is what FOLIO rewards.

The second manipulation varies the depth of the training proofs, and
Table~\ref{tab:depth} reports it. Trained on five-hop proofs, the formal
rendering writes accepted derivations on every five-hop problem and on about
a tenth of the fifteen-hop problems, whereas the condition trained at chain
length 25 stays above 99 percent everywhere. Benchmark accuracy is indifferent
to the training depth and lies between 46 and 51 percent on ProofWriter and
between 54 and 58 on FOLIO in every cell. The procedure therefore depends on
the depth of the training proofs while the correction of the bias does not.

\input{tab_depth2}

The third manipulation removes the instruction data. Trained on derivations
alone, in either rendering, the model writes a derivation the checker accepts
on every generated problem and scores zero on every benchmark, because when
asked a plain question it emits the opening token of the only format it knows
and stops. The derivations supply the procedure and the instruction mixture
supplies the ability to respond to an arbitrary prompt, and neither is
sufficient on its own.

The procedure can also be observed directly on a benchmark task. Web of lies
asks whether the last of a chain of people tells the truth, given a statement
about each person's honesty in terms of the previous one, so the answer is
obtained by carrying a truth value along the chain. Reading the value each
model carries at every position over all 250 items shows the control losing
it between the third and the fifth step, where its accuracy on the
intermediate value falls from 0.981 to 0.855, while the treated model retains
it, at 0.993 and 0.976. Every model answers correctly on essentially every
item whose intermediate values are all correct, so the accuracy gain on this
subtask is fully accounted for by the retained value.
Appendix~\ref{sec:wol-table} gives the full analysis.

\subsection{Chain-of-thought prompting}

If the improvement consisted of the model reasoning more, asking the control
to reason aloud would close the gap. Under a prompt that requests a chain of
reasoning before the answer, the Qwen2.5-7B control reaches 39.9 percent on
ProofWriter at depth 3, the formal conditions reach 45.3, 49.5 and 55.8
percent at 5, 10 and 25 percent of the mixture, and the English conditions
reach 44.1, 43.8 and 47.0. The advantage of the treated models is preserved
under the prompt, and for the formal rendering it grows with the share.

\subsection{Notation}
\label{sec:notation}

We find the English rendering to be the better default for three reasons.
It raises accuracy on every dense model measured, where the formal rendering
raises it on Qwen and lowers it on OLMo-2, and on the one sparse model both
renderings fail alike. It carries no measurable cost on aggregation tasks,
where the formal rendering costs 11 points on long-context word counting.
Finally, it extrapolates further beyond its training depth. Trained on
fifteen-hop proofs it writes accepted derivations on 99.5 percent of 25-hop
problems where the formal rendering manages 45 percent, and trained on
five-hop proofs it reaches 85.5 percent at fifteen hops where the formal
rendering reaches 13.3. A controlled study on the generator alone, reported in
Appendix~\ref{sec:crossover}, finds formal supervision ahead at the deepest
training band when the evaluation depth is held fixed. Performance at the
training depth and extrapolation beyond it therefore have to be reported
separately, and on the second the English rendering is ahead at every band.

\section{Checking the derivations}
\label{sec:substrate}

A derivation written in a fixed format can be checked by a program. Our
checker reads the premises of an item and accepts a proof line when the line
is a premise or follows from lines already established, in either notation.
On the generator's own problems, a derivation the checker accepts carries the
correct answer in 99 percent of samples, so selecting the first accepted
derivation out of sixteen matches the accuracy of asking whether any of the
sixteen is correct, without access to a gold answer. On ProofWriter the
checker reproduces the published gold label on 99, 95, 91, 91 and 84 percent
of items at depths 0 through 5, and its residual errors are answers of
``unknown'' on decided items, so it under-credits. An accepted derivation on
ProofWriter is correct between 53 and 69 percent of the time, because a third
of the items ask whether a claim is underivable and a forward chain never
demonstrates that. On GSM8K the coupling nearly disappears, since a wrong
answer there usually follows from correct arithmetic inside a wrong plan.

The coupling between validity and correctness is the property that training
against a verifier depends on, and it is usually obtained from a domain that
ships with a verifier, such as mathematics with a numeric answer or code with
a test suite \citep{lambert2025tulu, yue2025does}. Within the domain of the
generator, the trained procedure supplies the same property in the model's own
output, and a reward that pays for an accepted derivation is available for
every problem the generator can pose. We do not train against this reward in
the present paper, and the results above show that the coupling weakens as
soon as the checker is incomplete for the task, as it is on ProofWriter and
on GSM8K. We therefore regard the checkable derivations as a starting point
for verifier-based training on deduction and not as a general substitute for
task-specific verifiers.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

Four further generators built to match specific target tasks, namely a
program variable carried through assignment and branching, membership in a
reachable set with half the questions unreachable, a position in a temporal
order, and a quantity carried through unit conversions, improve none of their
targets when mixed at 5 percent. Temporal sequences on BIG-Bench Hard falls by
4 to 5 points in both renderings where the deduction data in English raises
it, HumanEval falls by 5.2 points, and long-context variable tracking does not
move. Matching the latent structure of a generator to a task is therefore not
sufficient to improve that task, and our results should not be read as a
general method for engineering capabilities from generated data.

The accuracy gains beyond ProofWriter, on FOLIO, on the chain subtasks of
BIG-Bench Hard and on the quantitative half of GPQA-Diamond, are measured on
Qwen2.5-7B and do not replicate on the other families, whose controls are
already at ceiling on the subtasks concerned. The procedure is installed only
partially on OLMo-2, and it is absent on the mixture of experts, which has one
seed and whose sharded training ran several times slower than a dense model of
its active size, so a pathology specific to sparse routing under our trainer
cannot be excluded. Our checker is sound but incomplete, since it cannot
certify that a fact is underivable. The models trained on the Tulu corpus are
verbose and do not reliably stop after a short answer, so their accuracies are
scored on partly degenerate text, although their answer distributions are
unambiguous and the within-corpus comparison is sound.

\section{Conclusion}

We have shown that a small fraction of program-generated deduction data,
substituted into an ordinary instruction mixture, improves an
instruction-tuned model on deduction and multi-step benchmarks well beyond
the generator's domain, and that 1 percent of the mixture is enough to
install a derivation-writing procedure that instruction tuning alone does not
produce. The gains arise from two separable mechanisms. The data teaches a
procedure for carrying a value through a chain of steps, which shuffled and
shallow proofs remove, and it corrects a bias in the answer distribution that
the instruction corpus installs, which we established by changing the corpus.
The second mechanism predicts the sign of the accuracy change across nine
treated models from three families, including the models on which the formal
rendering lowers accuracy, and the English rendering of the proofs improves
accuracy on every dense model we studied. We hope that these results
encourage the use of program-generated data as a complement to model-written
synthetic data, and that the diagnostic based on the answer distribution
proves useful in deciding when to apply it.
