\title{Garbage In, Performance Out: The Surprising Effect of Symbolically
Generated Deduction Data on LLMs}

\author{Anonymous authors}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Synthetic training data for language models is almost always written by
another language model, and its quality is bounded by the model that writes
it. We study the opposite extreme, a corpus of multi-hop deduction problems
and their solutions that a short program generates without any model in the
loop. The documents are correct by construction, but they carry no world
knowledge, use abstract constant names and apply a single inference rule
throughout. We replace a small fraction of a standard instruction-tuning
mixture with such documents while keeping the number of training examples
fixed, and we find that they improve the resulting model substantially. On
Qwen2.5-7B, replacing 5 to 10 percent of the mixture raises accuracy by 14
points on ProofWriter, 6 points on FOLIO, 7 points on the multi-step subtasks
of BIG-Bench Hard and up to 7 points on the quantitative items of
GPQA-Diamond, and it leaves general benchmarks unchanged. As little as 1
percent of the mixture teaches the model to write long derivations that a
symbolic checker accepts, an ability that no control model in our study has.
Two mechanisms account for the gains. The generated data teaches the model to
carry an intermediate value through a chain of steps, and it reduces a
systematic bias in the answers of instruction-tuned models that we trace to
the instruction corpus itself.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Synthetic data has become a standard ingredient in the training of language
models. Textbook-style corpora \citep{gunasekar2023textbooks,
eldan2023tinystories, abdin2024phi4}, rephrased web documents
\citep{maini2024rephrasing, maini2025beyondweb, su2025nemotroncc} and
prompted expansions of a seed corpus \citep{yang2025synthetic} have all been
shown to improve downstream performance. In nearly every case, however, the
synthetic text is written by another language model. Its quality is therefore
bounded by the knowledge and the reasoning of the model that writes it, and
repeated training on such text has been shown to narrow the distribution of
the trained model \citep{shumailov2024collapse, dohmatob2025strong}.

Synthetic data can also be written by a program. A generator of a few
hundred lines can sample a multi-hop deduction problem, solve it by forward
chaining and write the solution out, which yields documents that are correct
by construction and unlimited in number. Corpora of this kind have been used
to evaluate the reasoning of language models \citep{clark2020transformers,
tafjord2021proofwriter, saparov2023language} and to train models on deduction
in isolation \citep{morishita2023fld, morishita2024alt}. Whether they are
useful as an ingredient of general instruction tuning is much less clear. The
documents contain no world knowledge, they describe abstract constants with
arbitrary attributes, and they apply the same inference rule for dozens of
lines, so there is little reason to expect them to improve a model on
anything beyond the generator's own problems.

In this work we show that such data does improve a general instruction-tuned
model by a substantial margin, and we investigate why. We fine-tune base
models on a fixed instruction mixture of 100{,}000 examples in which a
fraction of the instruction examples is replaced by generated derivations.
Each derivation is written in one of two renderings of the same underlying
proof, either as a formal proof with declared symbols and numbered lines or
as a sequence of English sentences. On Qwen2.5-7B \citep{qwen2025qwen25},
replacing 5 to 10 percent of the mixture improves accuracy on ProofWriter
\citep{tafjord2021proofwriter} by 13.9 points, on FOLIO
\citep{han2024folio} by 6.4 points, on the multi-step subtasks of BIG-Bench
Hard by 6.7 points and on the quantitative items of GPQA-Diamond by up to
7.3 points, and it leaves general benchmarks unchanged. Replacing only 1
percent of the mixture is enough for this model to write derivations of
more than 50 lines that a symbolic checker accepts, at 1.8 times the chain
length seen in training, whereas no control model in our study writes an
accepted derivation.

We then ask how documents without content can have this effect, and we find
two mechanisms. First, the derivations teach the model to carry an
intermediate value through a chain of steps. Shuffling the lines of the
training proofs removes this effect, the depth of the training proofs bounds
how far the model can write derivations, and on the multi-step subtasks of BIG-Bench Hard we can observe the
untrained model losing the carried value midway through a chain. Second,
the derivations correct a systematic bias in the answers of the
instruction-tuned model. On ProofWriter the control model asserts that a
claim holds in three cases out of four, and the whole accuracy gain comes
from the negated claims that are false. We show that this bias originates in
the instruction corpus, since training the same base model on a second
instruction corpus produces the opposite bias, which the same derivations
correct in the opposite direction.

Our contributions are as follows. (i)~We show that a small fraction of
program-generated deduction data, substituted into an ordinary instruction
mixture, improves an instruction-tuned model on deduction and multi-step
benchmarks well beyond the generator's domain (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
(ii)~We show that the derivation-writing ability appears at 1 percent of
the mixture on Qwen2.5-7B and transfers to 3 model families
(Section~\ref{sec:families}). (iii)~We identify the two mechanisms behind
the gains and separate them by manipulating the order, the depth and the
presence of the training proofs and by changing the instruction corpus
(Sections~\ref{sec:ablations} and~\ref{sec:discussion}). (iv)~We find that the
English rendering of the proofs generalises further in depth and improves
ProofWriter accuracy on every model we study, whereas the formal rendering
helps some models and harms others (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Synthetic data in pretraining and post-training.} Synthetic text
written by language models now enters every stage of training. It forms a
large part of the pretraining corpus of Phi-4 \citep{abdin2024phi4} and
SmolLM2 \citep{benallal2025smollm2}, it is used to rephrase web text at scale
\citep{maini2024rephrasing, su2025nemotroncc}, and \citet{kang2025demystifying}
study its scaling behaviour systematically. Upsampling a domain at the end of
pretraining \citep{blakeney2024sparkjoy, liu2025midtraining,
wang2025octothinker} and the composition of the instruction mixture
\citep{lambert2025tulu, olmo2025olmo2} are known to have large effects on
downstream performance, which motivates our replacement design. The limits of
model-written data, both in the amount of human text available
\citep{villalobos2024position} and in the degradation caused by training on
model output \citep{shumailov2024collapse, dohmatob2025strong}, motivate
sources that involve no model.

\paragraph{Abstract and procedural data.} A line of work trains on data with
structure but without content. Pretraining on music or on artificial
languages transfers to natural language \citep{papadimitriou2020music,
chiang2022transferability, wu2022insights}, summarisation can be learned from
nonsense text \citep{krishna2021nonsense}, and pretraining on formal
languages imparts inductive biases that help later language learning
\citep{hu2025circuits}. \citet{jiang2026proceduralpretraining} warm up
language models on procedurally generated sequences before natural text, and
\citet{lacombe2026reasoningcore} provide a suite of procedural generators
for symbolic training. \citet{ruis2025procedural} find that the pretraining
documents that most influence a model's reasoning are those that show a
procedure. Our data is procedural in this sense, and we study it as a small
component of instruction tuning of a pretrained model.

\paragraph{Synthetic deduction data.} Program-generated deduction problems
have been used to test the reasoning of language models
\citep{clark2020transformers, tafjord2021proofwriter, saparov2023language,
saparov2023ood, zhu2024dyval} and to train them. FLD and ALT
\citep{morishita2023fld, morishita2024alt} train on large synthetic logic
corpora and report gains on logical reasoning benchmarks, LogiPT
\citep{feng2024logipt} trains a model to emulate a solver, and
\citet{xia2025formallogic} study whether formal logic can be learned from
data. \citet{zhang2023paradox} show that models trained on deduction data
can learn statistical shortcuts, which is a concern we address by
manipulating the proofs. \citet{zhou2025finelogic} compare symbolic and
English supervision on matched problems and \citet{kabra2026synthetic} show
that reinforcement learning on rule-generated multi-hop data improves
real-world multi-hop benchmarks. Recent reinforcement learning work uses
synthetic puzzles with verifiable answers as environments
\citep{xie2025logicrl, liu2025synlogic, chen2025enigmata,
stojanovski2025reasoninggym}. Our work differs from these in that the
generated data is a small replacement within an ordinary instruction
mixture, the evaluation is on general benchmarks, and the analysis is
directed at the mechanism of the gain.

\paragraph{Answer biases in tuned models.} Instruction-tuned models acquire
response biases that are separable from their underlying competence
\citep{ouyang2022training}, negation remains difficult for language models
\citep{kassner2020negated, hosseini2021understanding, truong2023language},
and shortcut learning is a general concern for trained models
\citep{geirhos2020shortcut}. We locate one such bias, attribute it to the
instruction corpus by manipulation, and show which training data corrects it.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Generated data}
\label{sec:data}

The generator samples a set of constants, a set of unary predicates and a set
of implication rules, chooses a target chain length, and constructs a problem
whose answer follows from the premises by that many applications of modus
ponens. It then solves the problem by forward chaining and writes the
solution out. Constants are named $c_0$ through $c_{25}$ and predicates are
drawn from a short list of colour and direction words, so that the documents
carry no world knowledge. The proof that the generator constructs is
written out in one of two renderings, both shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:overview-a}. The formal rendering
declares the constants and the predicate symbols, states the premises as
implications between atoms, and lists the proof as numbered lines together
with the rule that justifies each line. The English rendering states the
same premises and the same proof as ordinary sentences. The prompt, the
answer and the proof graph are common to the two renderings, so a comparison
between them isolates the effect of notation. The training documents have
chain lengths of up to 25 and the longest occupies 7{,}700 tokens.


\subsection{Training}

The instruction corpus is Dolci \citep{olmo2025olmo2}, the post-training
corpus of OLMo 3, from which we draw 100{,}000 single-turn examples. A
mixture with share $p$ replaces a fraction $p$ of these examples by generated
derivations, so that every model trains on the same number of examples. We
call the model trained on the unmodified 100{,}000 examples the control. We fine-tune each base model on each mixture for
one epoch with a global batch of 128 sequences and a context window of
8{,}192 tokens. Fewer than 0.5 percent of the examples exceed the window and
are truncated. The main sweep trains Qwen2.5-7B at shares of 1, 5, 10 and 25
percent in both renderings with 3 runs per configuration, and we report the
mean over runs throughout. The same recipe then trains
Qwen2.5-3B, Qwen2.5-14B, Qwen2.5-32B, Llama-3.1-8B and OLMo-2-7B at the
5 percent formal and 10 percent English shares, with the 32B model trained
on two nodes. All models are base models, since the
instruction tuning has to be identical across conditions for the comparison
to be meaningful. Appendix~\ref{sec:sweep-details} gives the remaining
hyperparameters.

\subsection{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

The evaluation covers ProofWriter under the open-world assumption at
inference depths 1, 3 and 5, on FOLIO, on GPQA-Diamond, on the three multi-hop question
answering datasets of LongBench and on BIG-Bench Hard. ProofWriter and FOLIO
are prompted for a direct answer, and we additionally evaluate ProofWriter
under a prompt that asks the model to reason before answering. GPQA-Diamond
is prompted to work through the options before the final choice, and
BIG-Bench Hard uses the standard three-shot chain-of-thought prompts. We
also evaluate on held-out problems of the generator, which we refer to as
\branchproof{}, at chain lengths 5 to 45. There the model is prompted in the
format on which it was trained and a forward-chaining checker verifies every
line of the derivation it writes, so the score is the fraction of problems
with an accepted derivation. Nine general benchmarks, among them GSM8K
\citep{cobbe2021training}, MMLU and HumanEval \citep{chen2021evaluating},
are described in Appendix~\ref{sec:general-table}. We audited
the overlap between the generated documents and the evaluation sets and
found no evaluation item that shares an eight-gram with any training
derivation.

For two benchmarks we additionally report a subset. BBH-chain averages the
eight subtasks of BIG-Bench Hard whose answer is obtained by propagating a
truth value or a state along a sequence of steps, namely web of lies, the
three object-tracking subtasks, the three logical-deduction subtasks and
formal fallacies. GPQA-quant consists of the 73 items of GPQA-Diamond whose
question contains a number with a physical unit or asks for a value, ratio
or factor. Appendix~\ref{sec:metrics} gives the exact rules.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Main results}

Table~\ref{tab:main} reports the results for Qwen2.5-7B across the replaced
share, and Figure~\ref{fig:overview-b} shows the formal rendering at 5
percent and the English rendering at 10 percent. Replacing 5 to 10 percent
of the instruction mixture with generated derivations improves every
deduction and multi-step benchmark. On
ProofWriter the English rendering at 10 percent improves accuracy by 6.4
points at depth 1, 13.9 points at depth 3 and 10.2 points at depth 5, and
the formal rendering at 5 percent improves it by 5.3, 10.7 and 7.7 points.
FOLIO improves by 6.4 points with the formal rendering at 5 percent. On
BIG-Bench Hard the macro average improves by 2.7 points, and the 8 chain
subtasks account for most of the gain, improving by 6.7 points while the
other 19 subtasks move by about 1 point. Web of lies, the chain subtask
closest in form to the generated data, improves by 10.4 points with the
formal rendering at 5 percent and by 2.0 points with the English rendering
at 10 percent. Appendix~\ref{sec:bbh-table} resolves every subtask.
GPQA-Diamond improves by up to 5.1 points, and the gain is concentrated in
the quantitative items, which improve by 4 to 7 points at the 5 and 10
percent shares and by 12.8 points at the 25 percent share of the formal
rendering, while the 125 remaining items move by less than 1 point. The
multi-hop question answering datasets improve by 1 to 3 points of F1
(Appendix~\ref{sec:multihop-table}). The general benchmarks in
Appendix~\ref{sec:general-table} neither improve nor degrade, with every
difference to the control within 3.5 points and most within 1.

The ability to write derivations that the checker accepts appears at a much
smaller share than the accuracy gains. The control writes no accepted
derivation on \branchproof{}, whereas the model trained with 1 percent
derivations writes one on 93 percent of problems at chain length 25 and on
90 percent at chain length 45, which is 1.8 times the longest chain in
training. This ability is essentially complete from 1 percent upward in both
renderings. Accuracy on the other benchmarks improves by about 1 point at 1
percent and reaches most of its value at 5 percent. Beyond that share the
two renderings differ. The English rendering peaks at 10 percent on every
benchmark, whereas the formal rendering keeps improving at 25 percent on
ProofWriter at depth 1 and under the reasoning prompt, on GPQA-Diamond and
on 2WikiMultihopQA. The two effects therefore have different thresholds.
Appendices~\ref{sec:folio-table} and~\ref{sec:depth-figure} resolve FOLIO
by gold label and ProofWriter by inference depth.

Table~\ref{tab:main} also compares direct answers on ProofWriter with
answers given after a requested chain of reasoning. The control gains 0.6
points from reasoning aloud, so the gap to the models trained with
derivations is not closed by prompting. For the formal rendering the score
under the reasoning prompt grows with the share and reaches 55.8 percent at
25 percent, whereas for the English rendering the direct answer is more
accurate at every share.

\subsection{Model families and scales}
\label{sec:families}

Table~\ref{tab:family} repeats the experiment on Qwen2.5-3B, Qwen2.5-14B,
Qwen2.5-32B, Llama-3.1-8B and OLMo-2-7B with both renderings. The
derivation-writing ability transfers to every Qwen and Llama model. The
controls below 14B write no accepted derivation at any chain length, the
14B control reaches 7.5 percent at chain length 25 by reasoning in prose,
and every Qwen and Llama model trained with derivations writes accepted
derivations on 93 to 100 percent of problems at every chain length. OLMo-2 acquires the ability only in part, on about a
fifth of the problems at chain length 5 as well as 25, so its shorter
context does not explain the gap.

The gain on ProofWriter transfers to every model. With the English
rendering it is 3.2 points on Qwen2.5-3B, 10.6 on Qwen2.5-14B, 15.5 on
Llama-3.1-8B and 10.8 on OLMo-2-7B, and with the formal rendering it is 3.9,
10.7, 6.2 and 8.6 points on the 4 Qwen models from 3B to 32B and 3.0 points
on Llama-3.1-8B, where the 32B model also improves by 21.2 points at
inference depth 5. The formal
rendering lowers ProofWriter accuracy on OLMo-2 by 12.2 points. FOLIO and GPQA-Diamond move
by less than 3 points on the other models, except for GPQA-Diamond on
Qwen2.5-14B, which improves by 4.5 points. The controls of these models
already score at or near 100 percent on the chain subtasks of BIG-Bench
Hard, so the gains on that benchmark observed on Qwen2.5-7B correspond to a
deficit of the 7B model that the others do not have.
Section~\ref{sec:prior} explains the loss of OLMo-2 under the formal
rendering.

\subsection{Ablations}
\label{sec:ablations}

Three ablations on Qwen2.5-7B separate the two effects of the derivations.
The first shuffles the lines of every training proof, which preserves every
token, the vocabulary, the length and the conclusion, and destroys only the
order in which the facts follow. These runs replace 10 percent of the
mixture and are compared with a length-matched control that replaces the
same share by ordinary long documents. On ProofWriter at depth 3 the ordered
English derivations improve accuracy by 12.6 points over the control and the
shuffled ones by 7.8 points, compared with 1.0 point for the length-matched
control, so about 3 fifths of the improvement survives. On FOLIO almost none
of it does, since the ordered English derivations improve accuracy by 5.4
points and the shuffled ones by 1.5 points. The model trained
on shuffled derivations also writes no derivation that the checker accepts.

The second ablation varies the depth of the training proofs, and
Table~\ref{tab:depth} reports it. Trained on 5-hop proofs, the model
writes accepted derivations on every 5-hop problem but on only about a
tenth of the 15-hop problems with the formal rendering, whereas the
model trained on proofs of up to 25 hops stays above 99 percent everywhere.
Benchmark accuracy is indifferent to the training depth and lies between 46
and 51 percent on ProofWriter and between 54 and 58 percent on FOLIO in every
cell.

\input{tab_depth2}

The third ablation removes the instruction data. Trained on derivations
alone, in either rendering, the model writes a derivation the checker
accepts on every generator problem and scores zero on every benchmark,
because when asked a plain question it emits the opening token of the only
format it knows and stops. The derivations and the instruction data are
therefore both necessary.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Answer distribution}
\label{sec:prior}

ProofWriter presents a theory together with a claim and asks whether the
claim is true, false or unknown. Table~\ref{tab:mechanism} splits the
decidable items by the polarity of the claim and by the gold label. The
control is almost always right when the claim is true and mostly wrong when
the claim is negated and false, where its accuracy is 17.6 percent, because
it asserts the claim in three cases out of four regardless of its content.
The derivations move the answer distribution toward the gold labels, and
the improvement is concentrated in the negated and false claims, whose
accuracy rises by 39.3 points to 56.9 percent at the 25 percent share. The
negated and true cell loses 4.8 points and the positive and true cell 1.3
points, and the positive and false cell fluctuates within 3 points of the
control. In a typical item the theory contains
``If someone is young then they visit the dog. Red people are young. If the
rabbit visits the dog then the dog is red.'' together with facts from which
the rabbit is red, and the claim is ``The dog is not red''. The control
answers ``true'' and the models trained with derivations answer ``false''.
FOLIO shows the same pattern, with the false and uncertain items gaining and
the true ones fixed, and Appendix~\ref{sec:examples} gives further items
from both benchmarks. A log-likelihood probe in
Appendix~\ref{sec:probe-table} shows that the change is mostly a shift of
the decision margin on negated claims, since the probability that the model
prefers ``true'' falls from 64.0 to 48.0 percent, and to a smaller extent a gain in
discrimination, since the area under the ranking curve over all decidable
items rises from 66.2 to 77.0 percent with the English rendering. Two perturbation
probes in Appendix~\ref{sec:perturbation} confirm that most of the
additional ``false'' answers depend on the premises.

\input{tab_mech_main}

This account also explains the loss on OLMo-2 in Section~\ref{sec:families}.
Across the models of Tables~\ref{tab:family} and~\ref{tab:corpus}, the
change in accuracy on ProofWriter follows how far the derivations move the
answer distribution toward or away from the gold labels. The OLMo-2 control
is already close to the gold distribution, since its most frequent answer
exceeds the gold frequency of that label by 8.6 points, and the formal
derivations push it 46 points toward ``unknown'', so its accuracy falls,
whereas the English derivations move it toward the labels and its accuracy
rises. On every model, the English rendering moves the answer distribution
toward the labels. Since the answer distribution of the control
can be measured before any derivations are added, it indicates in advance
whether the derivations will help a given model and in which rendering.

\subsection{Intermediate values in multi-step tasks}
\label{sec:procedure}

The ablations show that the gains on FOLIO require ordered proofs, and
that the depth of the training proofs bounds how far the model can write
derivations. The procedure that the derivations teach can also be observed
directly on a benchmark task. Web of lies, the subtask of BIG-Bench Hard
closest in form to the generated data, states for each of 5 people whether
they tell the truth given the previous person, so the answer is obtained by
carrying a truth value along the chain. The formal rendering at 10 percent
improves this subtask by 12.1 points, whereas the English rendering improves
it by at most 3.5 points at any share. The model writes 1 numbered line per
person, so we read the truth value it carries at every position and compare
it with the value the chain implies, over all 250 items. The control is
correct on the intermediate value at the third step on 98 percent of items
and at the fifth step on 86 percent, so it loses the value along the chain,
whereas the model trained with formal derivations is correct on 99 and 98
percent. Every model answers correctly on essentially every item whose
intermediate values are all correct, so the gain on this subtask is fully
accounted for by the retained value.
The item below shows the failure in the control and its absence after
training. The two chains of thought are identical up to step 3, where the
control misreads its own conclusion of step 2 and carries the wrong value to
the end.

\begin{small}
\begin{quote}
\textbf{Question.} Vina tells the truth. Helene says Vina lies. Kandi says
Helene tells the truth. Jamey says Kandi lies. Ka says Jamey lies. Does Ka
tell the truth?

\textbf{Control.} (1) Vina tells the truth. (2) Since Vina tells the truth
and Helene says Vina lies, Helene lies. (3) Since we know from (2) that
Helene \emph{tells the truth}, and Kandi says Helene tells the truth, Kandi
tells the truth. (4) Jamey lies. (5) Ka tells the truth. So the answer is
Yes.

\textbf{With 10 percent formal derivations.} (1) Vina tells the truth. (2)
Helene lies. (3) Since we know from (2) that Helene lies, and Kandi says
Helene tells the truth, Kandi lies. (4) Jamey tells the truth. (5) Ka lies.
So the answer is No.
\end{quote}
\end{small}

Appendix~\ref{sec:wol-table} gives the full analysis, the tracking
subtasks show the same pattern, and Appendix~\ref{sec:examples} gives
items from every benchmark.

\subsection{Notation}

On ProofWriter and on extrapolation in depth, the English rendering is
the better default on the evidence of this paper. It improves ProofWriter
accuracy on every model we study, whereas the formal rendering improves Qwen
and Llama and harms OLMo-2. It generalises further beyond its training
depth, since trained on 15-hop proofs it writes accepted derivations on 99.5
percent of 25-hop problems where the formal rendering reaches 45 percent,
and trained on 5-hop proofs it reaches 85.5 percent at 15 hops where the
formal rendering reaches 13.3 percent. The formal rendering is ahead on web
of lies and under the reasoning prompt on ProofWriter, and a controlled
study on the generator alone, reported in Appendix~\ref{sec:crossover},
finds it ahead when the training proofs are deepest and the evaluation
depth is held fixed. The two renderings should therefore be compared
separately at the training depth and beyond it.

\subsection{Verification of derivations}

A derivation in the trained format is
checked by the same program that wrote the training data. On the generator's
own problems, a derivation the checker accepts carries the correct answer in
99 percent of samples, so selecting the first accepted derivation out of
16 matches the accuracy of selecting with the gold answer. On
ProofWriter the checker reproduces the published label on 84 to 99 percent
of items depending on the depth, and an accepted derivation is correct
between 53 and 69 percent of the time, because a third of the items ask
whether a claim is underivable and a forward chain cannot show that. The
coupling between validity and correctness therefore holds where the checker
is complete for the task, which makes the trained format a candidate for
verifier-based training on deduction \citep{lambert2025tulu, yue2025does},
although we do not pursue this here.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

The gains beyond ProofWriter are established on Qwen2.5-7B, and the other
models we study either lack the corresponding deficit or have not been
evaluated on every benchmark. Only the Qwen2.5-7B runs are replicated, and
the 32B model is trained once with the formal rendering only. The models
range from 3B to 32B parameters and the instruction mixtures are 100{,}000
examples, so the effect of the derivations under longer post-training
remains open. We compare the generated data with ordinary instruction data
and with length-matched documents but not with synthetic deduction data
written by a language model. Appendix~\ref{sec:midtrain-detail} reports
the same data used in midtraining, where it produces the same gain on
ProofWriter at a higher cost. The
checker is sound but incomplete, since it cannot certify that a fact is
underivable, which limits its use on benchmarks with unknown labels. Finally,
the two renderings differ in their effect on OLMo-2, and we explain this
through the answer distribution but have not identified what in the
pretraining of that model produces its different starting point.

\section{Conclusion}

Deduction data written by a program, which carries no world knowledge and
applies a single inference rule, improves an instruction-tuned model when
it replaces a small fraction of the instruction mixture. Two separable
mechanisms produce the gains. The data teaches the model to carry a value
through a chain of steps, which shuffling the proofs removes, and it
reduces a bias in the answer distribution that the instruction corpus
produces, which we established by changing the corpus. The English
rendering of the proofs improved ProofWriter accuracy on every model we
studied, and the answer distribution of the control predicted the direction
of the change in every case. Program-generated data is therefore a usable
complement to model-written synthetic data in post-training, at least for
deduction.
