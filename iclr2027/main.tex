\section{Introduction}

Synthetic data has become a standard ingredient in the training of language
models. Textbook-style corpora \citep{gunasekar2023textbooks,
eldan2023tinystories, abdin2024phi4}, rephrased web documents
\citep{maini2024rephrasing, maini2025beyondweb, su2025nemotroncc} and
prompted expansions of a seed corpus \citep{yang2025synthetic} have all been
shown to improve downstream performance. In nearly every case, however, the
synthetic text is written by another language model. Its quality is therefore
bounded by the knowledge and the reasoning of the model that writes it, and
repeated training on such text has been shown to narrow the distribution of
the trained model \citep{shumailov2024collapse, dohmatob2025strong}.

Synthetic data can also be produced by a program. A generator of a few
hundred lines can sample a multi-hop deduction problem, solve it by forward
chaining and write the solution out, which yields documents that are correct
by construction and unlimited in number. Corpora of this kind have been used
to evaluate the reasoning of language models \citep{clark2020transformers,
tafjord2021proofwriter, saparov2023language} and to train models on deduction
in isolation \citep{morishita2023fld, morishita2024alt}. Whether they are
useful as an ingredient of general instruction tuning is much less clear. The
documents contain no world knowledge, they describe abstract constants with
arbitrary attributes, and they apply the same inference rule for dozens of
lines, so there is little reason to expect them to improve a model on
anything beyond the generator's own problems.

In this work we show that such data does improve a general instruction-tuned
model by a substantial margin, and investigate the causes behind this. We fine-tune base
models on an instruction mixture of fixed size in which a
fraction of the instruction examples is replaced by generated derivations.
Each derivation is written in one of two renderings of the same underlying
proof, either as a formal proof with declared symbols and numbered lines or
as a sequence of English sentences. On Qwen2.5-7B \citep{qwen2025qwen25},
replacing 5 to 10 percent of the mixture improves accuracy on ProofWriter
\citep{tafjord2021proofwriter} by 13.9 points, on FOLIO
\citep{han2024folio} by 6.4 points, on the multi-step subtasks of BIG-Bench
Hard by 6.7 points and on the quantitative items of GPQA-Diamond by up to
7.3 points, and it leaves general benchmarks unchanged. Replacing only 1
percent of the mixture is enough for this model to write derivations of
more than 50 lines that a symbolic checker accepts, at 1.8 times the chain
length seen in training, whereas no baseline model in our study writes an
accepted derivation. Section~\ref{sec:families} repeats the comparison on
Qwen2.5 at 3B, 7B and 32B, on Qwen3-30B-A3B, on Llama-3.1-8B and on
OLMo-2-7B.

We then ask how documents without content can have this effect, and we find
two mechanisms. First, the derivations teach the model to carry an
intermediate value through a chain of steps, as we show by shuffling the
lines of the training proofs, which removes the effect, and by observing the
baseline model lose the carried value midway through a chain on the
multi-step subtasks of BIG-Bench Hard. Second,
the derivations correct a systematic bias in the answers of the
instruction-tuned model. On ProofWriter the baseline model asserts that a
claim holds in three cases out of four, and the whole accuracy gain comes
from the negated claims that are false. This bias originates in the instruction corpus. Tuning the base model on a
second instruction corpus leaves it asserting that the claim fails in four
cases out of five, and adding the derivations then moves its answers back
toward the labels from the other side.

Our contributions are as follows. (i)~We show that a small fraction of
program-generated deduction data, substituted into an ordinary instruction
mixture, improves an instruction-tuned model on deduction and multi-step
benchmarks well beyond the generator's domain (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
(ii)~We show that the derivation-writing ability appears at 1 percent of
the mixture on Qwen2.5-7B and that it and the gains transfer to
3 model families and to models from 3B to 32B parameters
(Section~\ref{sec:families}). (iii)~We identify the two mechanisms behind
the gains and separate them by manipulating the order, the depth and the
presence of the training proofs and by changing the instruction corpus
(Sections~\ref{sec:ablations} and~\ref{sec:discussion}). (iv)~We find that the
English rendering of the proofs generalises further in depth and improves
ProofWriter accuracy on every model we study, whereas the formal rendering
helps some models and harms others (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Synthetic data in pretraining and post-training.} Synthetic text
written by language models now enters every stage of training. It forms a
large part of the pretraining corpus of Phi-4 \citep{abdin2024phi4} and
SmolLM2 \citep{benallal2025smollm2}, it is used to rephrase web text at scale
\citep{maini2024rephrasing, su2025nemotroncc}, and its scaling behaviour has
been studied systematically \citep{kang2025demystifying}. Upsampling a domain at the end of
pretraining \citep{blakeney2024sparkjoy, liu2025midtraining,
wang2025octothinker} and the composition of the instruction mixture
\citep{lambert2025tulu, olmo2025olmo2} are known to have large effects on
downstream performance, which motivates our replacement design. The limits of
model-written data, both in the amount of human text available
\citep{villalobos2024position} and in the degradation caused by training on
model output \citep{shumailov2024collapse, dohmatob2025strong}, motivate
sources that involve no model.

\paragraph{Abstract and procedural data.} A line of work trains on data with
structure but without content. Pretraining on music or on artificial
languages transfers to natural language \citep{papadimitriou2020music,
chiang2022transferability, wu2022insights}, summarisation can be learned from
nonsense text \citep{krishna2021nonsense}, and pretraining on formal
languages imparts inductive biases that help later language learning
\citep{hu2025circuits}. \citet{jiang2026proceduralpretraining} warm up
language models on procedurally generated sequences before natural text, and
\citet{lacombe2026reasoningcore} provide a suite of procedural generators
for symbolic training. \citet{ruis2025procedural} find that the pretraining
documents that most influence a model's reasoning are those that show a
procedure. Our data is procedural in this sense, and we study it as a small
component of instruction tuning of a pretrained model.

\paragraph{Synthetic deduction data.} Program-generated deduction problems
have been used to test the reasoning of language models
\citep{clark2020transformers, tafjord2021proofwriter, saparov2023language,
saparov2023ood, zhu2024dyval} and to train them. FLD and ALT
\citep{morishita2023fld, morishita2024alt} train on large synthetic logic
corpora and report gains on logical reasoning benchmarks, LogiPT
\citep{feng2024logipt} trains a model to emulate a solver, and
\citet{xia2025formallogic} study whether formal logic can be learned from
data. \citet{zhang2023paradox} show that models trained on deduction data
can learn statistical shortcuts, which is a concern we address by
manipulating the proofs. \citet{zhou2025finelogic} compare symbolic and
English supervision on matched problems and \citet{kabra2026synthetic} show
that reinforcement learning on rule-generated multi-hop data improves
real-world multi-hop benchmarks. Recent reinforcement learning work uses
synthetic puzzles with verifiable answers as environments
\citep{xie2025logicrl, liu2025synlogic, chen2025enigmata,
stojanovski2025reasoninggym}. Our work differs from these in that the
generated data is a small replacement within an ordinary instruction
mixture, the evaluation is on general benchmarks, and the analysis is
directed at the mechanism of the gain.

\paragraph{Answer biases in tuned models.} Instruction-tuned models acquire
response biases that are separable from their underlying competence
\citep{ouyang2022training}, negation remains difficult for language models
\citep{kassner2020negated, hosseini2021understanding, truong2023language},
and shortcut learning is a general concern for trained models
\citep{geirhos2020shortcut}. We locate one such bias, attribute it to the
instruction corpus and show which training data corrects it.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Generated data}
\label{sec:data}

Our generator samples a set of constants, a set of unary predicates and a set
of implication rules, chooses a target chain length, and constructs a problem
whose answer follows from the premises by that many applications of modus
ponens. It then solves the problem by forward chaining and writes the
solution out. Constants are named $c_0$ through $c_{25}$ and predicates are
drawn from a short list of colour and direction words, so that the documents
carry no world knowledge. The proof that the generator constructs is
written out in one of two renderings, both shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:overview}. The formal rendering
declares the constants and the predicate symbols, states the premises as
implications between atoms, and lists the proof as numbered lines together
with the rule that justifies each line. The English rendering states the
same premises and the same proof as ordinary sentences. The prompt, the
answer and the proof graph are common to the two renderings, so a comparison
between them isolates the effect of notation. The training documents have
chain lengths of up to 25 and the longest occupies 7{,}700 tokens.


\subsection{Training}

We use Dolci \citep{olmo2025olmo2} as our main instruction corpus, the post-training
corpus of OLMo 3, from which we draw 100{,}000 single-turn examples, and we
repeat the main comparison on a second corpus in Section~\ref{sec:corpus}. A mixture with share $p$ replaces a uniformly sampled fraction $p$ of these
examples by generated derivations, so that every model trains on the same
number of examples, and the baseline trains on the unmodified 100{,}000
examples. We fine-tune each base model on each mixture for one epoch with a
global batch of 128 sequences and a context window of 8{,}192 tokens, which
fewer than 0.5 percent of the examples exceed. The main sweep trains
Qwen2.5-7B at shares of 1, 5, 10 and 25 percent in both renderings with 3
runs per configuration, and we report the mean over runs throughout. In the
same way we train Qwen2.5-3B, Qwen2.5-14B, Qwen2.5-32B, Llama-3.1-8B and
OLMo-2-7B at the 5 percent formal and 10 percent English shares. All models
are base models, which keeps the instruction tuning identical across
conditions. Appendix~\ref{sec:sweep-details} gives the remaining
hyperparameters.

\subsection{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

The evaluation covers ProofWriter under the open-world assumption at
inference depths 1, 3 and 5, on FOLIO, on GPQA-Diamond, on the three multi-hop question
answering datasets of LongBench and on BIG-Bench Hard. ProofWriter and FOLIO are prompted for a direct answer, and we additionally
evaluate ProofWriter under a prompt that asks the model to reason before
answering. GPQA-Diamond is prompted to work through the options before the
final choice, and BIG-Bench Hard uses the standard three-shot
chain-of-thought prompts. We also evaluate on held-out problems of the
generator, which we refer to as \branchproof{}, at chain lengths 5 to 45,
where the model is prompted in the format on which it was trained and a
forward-chaining checker verifies every line of the derivation it writes, so
the score is the fraction of problems with an accepted derivation. Nine
general benchmarks, among them GSM8K \citep{cobbe2021training}, MMLU and
HumanEval \citep{chen2021evaluating}, are described in
Appendix~\ref{sec:general-table}. No evaluation item shares an eight-gram
with any training derivation.

For two benchmarks we additionally report a subset. BBH-chain averages the
eight subtasks of BIG-Bench Hard whose answer is obtained by propagating a
truth value or a state along a sequence of steps, namely web of lies, the
three object-tracking subtasks, the three logical-deduction subtasks and
formal fallacies, and GPQA-quant consists of the 73 items of GPQA-Diamond
whose question contains a number with a physical unit or asks for a value,
ratio or factor (Appendix~\ref{sec:metrics}).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Main results}

Table~\ref{tab:main} reports the results for Qwen2.5-7B across the replaced
share, and Figure~\ref{fig:overview} shows the formal rendering at 5
percent and the English rendering at 10 percent. Replacing 5 to 10 percent
of the instruction mixture with generated derivations improves every
deduction and multi-step benchmark. On
ProofWriter the English rendering at 10 percent improves accuracy by 6.4
points at depth 1, 13.9 points at depth 3 and 10.2 points at depth 5, and
the formal rendering at 5 percent improves it by 5.3, 10.7 and 7.7 points.
FOLIO improves by 6.4 points with the formal rendering at 5 percent. On BIG-Bench Hard the macro average improves by 2.7 points and the 8 chain
subtasks account for most of the gain, improving by 6.7 points while the
other 19 subtasks move by about 1 point, and web of lies, the subtask
closest in form to the generated data, improves by 10.4 points with the
formal rendering at 5 percent (Appendix~\ref{sec:bbh-table}). GPQA-Diamond
improves by up to 5.1 points, and the gain is concentrated in the
quantitative items, which improve by 4 to 7 points at the 5 and 10 percent
shares and by 12.8 points at the 25 percent share of the formal rendering,
while the 125 remaining items move by less than 1 point. The multi-hop
question answering datasets improve by 1 to 3 points of F1
(Appendix~\ref{sec:multihop-table}) and the general benchmarks in
Appendix~\ref{sec:general-table} neither improve nor degrade.

The ability to write derivations that the checker accepts appears at a much
smaller share than the accuracy gains. The baseline writes no accepted
derivation on \branchproof{}, whereas the model trained with 1 percent
derivations writes one on 93 percent of problems at chain length 25 and on
90 percent at chain length 45, which is 1.8 times the longest chain in
training, and this ability is essentially complete from 1 percent upward in
both renderings. Accuracy on the other benchmarks improves by about 1 point
at 1 percent and reaches most of its value at 5 percent, and beyond that
share the two renderings differ. The English rendering peaks at 10 percent on every
benchmark, whereas the formal rendering keeps improving at 25 percent on
ProofWriter at depth 1 and under the reasoning prompt, on GPQA-Diamond and
on 2WikiMultihopQA. The two effects therefore have different thresholds.
Appendices~\ref{sec:folio-table} and~\ref{sec:depth-figure} resolve FOLIO
by gold label and ProofWriter by inference depth.
Each mixture here carries a single rendering. Appendix~\ref{sec:mixed-table}
trains on mixtures that carry both renderings and finds that the transfer
follows the share while one added sentence naming the notation is what lets
a single model write an accepted derivation in either rendering on request.

Table~\ref{tab:main} also compares direct answers on ProofWriter with
answers given after a requested chain of reasoning. The baseline gains 0.6
points from reasoning aloud, so prompting does not close the gap to the
models trained with derivations. For the formal rendering the score under
the reasoning prompt grows with the share and reaches 55.8 percent, whereas
for the English rendering the direct answer is more accurate at every share.

\subsection{Model families and scales}
\label{sec:families}

Table~\ref{tab:family} repeats the experiment on Qwen2.5-3B, Qwen2.5-14B,
Qwen2.5-32B, Llama-3.1-8B and OLMo-2-7B with both renderings. The
derivation-writing ability transfers to every Qwen and Llama model. The
baselines below 14B write no accepted derivation at any chain length, the
14B baseline reaches 7.5 percent at chain length 25 by reasoning in prose,
and every Qwen and Llama model trained with derivations writes accepted
derivations on 93 to 100 percent of problems at every chain length. OLMo-2 acquires the ability only in part, on about a
fifth of the problems at chain length 5 as well as 25, so its shorter
context does not explain the gap.

The gain on ProofWriter transfers to every model. With the English rendering
it is 3.2 points on Qwen2.5-3B, 10.6 on Qwen2.5-14B, 15.5 on Llama-3.1-8B
and 10.8 on OLMo-2-7B, where the English run of Qwen2.5-32B is still
training, and with the formal rendering it is 3.9, 10.7, 6.2 and 8.6 points
on the 4 Qwen models from 3B to 32B and 3.0 points on Llama-3.1-8B. The
formal rendering lowers ProofWriter accuracy on OLMo-2 by 12.2 points,
because it moves an answer distribution that was already close to the labels
onto "unknown", an effect Section~\ref{sec:prior} develops in more detail.
FOLIO and GPQA-Diamond move by less than 3 points on the other models,
except for GPQA-Diamond on Qwen2.5-14B, which improves by 4.5 points. The
baselines of these models already score at or near 100 percent on the chain
subtasks of BIG-Bench Hard, so the gain there on Qwen2.5-7B repairs a
deficit the others do not have.

\subsection{Ablations}
\label{sec:ablations}

Four further sets of experiments on Qwen2.5-7B ask which properties of the
derivations matter. We vary the instruction corpus they are mixed into,
which tests whether the gain depends on that corpus, and we vary the order
of the lines within each derivation, the depth of the proofs and whether any
instruction data is present at all, which separate the two effects that the
derivations have.

\paragraph{A second instruction corpus.}
\label{sec:corpus}
Table~\ref{tab:corpus} repeats the experiment with 100{,}000 single-turn
examples of Tulu 3 \citep{lambert2025tulu} in place of Dolci. The two
corpora leave the baseline with opposite biases. The model tuned on Dolci
answers "true" on 73 percent of the ProofWriter items and the model tuned on
Tulu 3 answers "false" on 82 percent, while the gold labels are 40 percent
true and 43 percent false. Replacing 5 percent of either corpus by formal
derivations moves the answers toward the labels in both cases, by 23 points
on Dolci and by 12 on Tulu 3, and teaches the derivation procedure on both,
with the checker accepting 97.5 percent of the derivations the Tulu 3
mixture writes at chain length 25 and none of those the baseline writes. The
accuracy that follows from the correction does not. On Dolci the mixture
gains 10.7 points at inference depth 3, 7.7 at depth 5 and 6.4 on FOLIO, and
on Tulu 3 it loses 0.8, 3.2 and 2.5 points on the same benchmarks. The Tulu
3 baseline is already 17 points more accurate at inference depth 3, so there
is little room left to repair. The correction therefore does not depend on
one corpus, and it turns into accuracy only where the corpus leaves a bias
large enough to cost accuracy.

\input{tab_corpus}

\paragraph{Shuffled lines.}
Table~\ref{tab:shuffle} shuffles the lines of every training derivation,
which preserves every token, the length and the conclusion and destroys
only the order in which the facts follow. These runs enter the derivations during
midtraining at a 10 percent share (Appendix~\ref{sec:midtrain-detail}) and
are compared with a length-matched baseline that replaces the same share by
ordinary long documents. On ProofWriter at inference depth 3 the ordered
English derivations improve accuracy by 12.6 points over the baseline and
the shuffled ones by 7.8, against 1.0 for the length-matched baseline, and
on FOLIO by 4.4 points in English and 5.4 in the formal rendering against
1.5 for the shuffled ones. The model trained on shuffled derivations writes
no derivation that the checker accepts. The order of the lines is therefore
what teaches the derivation-writing procedure, while the tokens alone
account for most of the ProofWriter gain.

\input{tab_shuffle}

\paragraph{Training depth.}
Table~\ref{tab:depth} varies the depth of the training proofs. Trained on 5-hop proofs, the model writes accepted derivations on every
5-hop problem but on only about a tenth of the 15-hop problems with the
formal rendering, whereas the model trained on proofs of up to 25 hops stays
above 99 percent everywhere. Benchmark accuracy is indifferent to the
training depth.

\paragraph{Derivations alone.}
Trained on derivations alone, in either rendering, the model writes a
derivation the checker accepts on every generator problem and scores zero on
every benchmark, because when asked a plain question it emits the opening
token of the only format it knows and stops
(Table~\ref{tab:alone}, Appendix~\ref{sec:alone-table}). The derivations and
the instruction data are both necessary.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Appendix~\ref{sec:examples} gives benchmark items that the baseline answers
incorrectly together with the answers of the trained models.

\subsection{Answer distribution}
\label{sec:prior}

ProofWriter presents a theory together with a claim and asks whether the
claim is true, false or unknown. Table~\ref{tab:mechanism} splits the
decidable items by the polarity of the claim and by the gold label. The baseline
is almost always right when the claim is true and mostly wrong when the
claim is negated and false, where its accuracy is 17.6 percent, because it
asserts the claim in three cases out of four regardless of its content. The
derivations move the answer distribution toward the gold labels, and the
improvement is concentrated in the negated and false claims, whose accuracy
rises by 39.3 points to 56.9 percent at the 25 percent share, while the
other 3 cells move by less than 5 points. In a typical item the theory contains
"If someone is young then they visit the dog. Red people are young. If the
rabbit visits the dog then the dog is red." together with facts from which
the rabbit is red, and the claim is "The dog is not red". The baseline
answers "true" and the models trained with derivations answer "false".
FOLIO shows the same pattern, with the false and uncertain items gaining and
the true ones fixed. A log-likelihood probe
(Appendix~\ref{sec:probe-table}) shows that the change is mostly a shift of
the decision margin on negated claims and to a smaller extent a gain in
discrimination, and two perturbation probes
(Appendix~\ref{sec:perturbation}) confirm that most of the additional "false"
answers depend on the premises.

\input{tab_mech_main}

This account also explains the loss on OLMo-2 in
Section~\ref{sec:families}. Across the models of
Tables~\ref{tab:family} and~\ref{tab:corpus}, the change in ProofWriter
accuracy follows how far the derivations move the answer distribution toward
or away from the gold labels. The OLMo-2 baseline is already close to the gold distribution and the formal
derivations push it 46 points toward "unknown", so its accuracy falls,
whereas the English derivations move it toward the labels on every model and
its accuracy rises. Since that distribution can be measured before any
derivations are added, it indicates in advance whether they will help a given
model and in which rendering.

\subsection{Intermediate values in multi-step tasks}
\label{sec:procedure}

The procedure can also be observed directly on a benchmark task. Web of lies,
the subtask of BIG-Bench Hard closest in form to the generated data, states
for five people whether they tell the truth given the previous person, so the
answer is obtained by carrying a truth value along the chain. The formal
rendering at 10 percent improves it by 12.1 points and the English rendering
by at most 3.5 points at any share. The model writes one numbered line per
person, so we read the value it carries at every position over all 250 items.
The baseline is correct at the third step on 98 percent of items and at the
fifth on 86 percent, so it loses the value along the chain, whereas the model
trained with formal derivations is correct on 99 and 98 percent
(Figure~\ref{fig:steps}). Every model answers correctly on essentially every
item whose intermediate values are all correct, so the gain is fully
accounted for by the retained value. Appendix~\ref{sec:wol-example}
reproduces an item on which the baseline misreads its own conclusion of step
2 and carries the wrong value to the end.

every position for every share, and the object-tracking subtasks behave in
the same way.

\subsection{Notation}

The English rendering is the better default here. It improves ProofWriter accuracy on every model we study, whereas the formal
rendering improves Qwen and Llama and harms OLMo-2, and trained on 15-hop
proofs it writes accepted derivations on 99.5 percent of 25-hop problems
against 45 percent for the formal rendering. The formal rendering is ahead
on web of lies and, in Appendix~\ref{sec:crossover}, on the generator when
the training proofs are deepest.
Accepted derivations carry the correct answer on 99 percent of the
generator's problems (Appendix~\ref{sec:verification}).

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

The instruction mixtures are 100{,}000 examples throughout, so the effect at
other mixture sizes and under longer post-training remains open, and we do not
test whether the gains survive reinforcement learning or compare with
deduction data written by a language model. We explain the effect of the two
renderings on OLMo-2 through its answer distribution without identifying what
in its pretraining produces that distribution.

\section{Conclusion}

Deduction data written by a program, which carries no world knowledge and
applies a single inference rule, improves an instruction-tuned model when it
replaces a small fraction of the instruction mixture. Two separable mechanisms
produce the gains. The data teaches the model to carry a value along a chain
of steps, which shuffling removes, and it corrects a bias in the answer
distribution.
